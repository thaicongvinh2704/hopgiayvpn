A rectangular flatbread pizza box is a custom carton concept for elongated pizzas, flatbreads and similar bakery or food-service items that do not fit efficiently in a square pizza box. The supplied image set shows a long, shallow shape with a printed top and open interior. It does not provide finished measurements, board specification or a tested production sample. The key design question is how much usable length and width the actual product needs, including its crust edges, topping profile, serving board and handling clearance.

For an elongated product, a few millimeters of difference can affect end clearance, presentation and how easily a staff member lifts the food out. A square format may leave unused corners; an overly close rectangular fit can scrape toppings or catch the crust at the ends. This page sets out the information a buyer and packaging supplier should resolve together. The images are concept visualizations, not verified production samples and not evidence of food-contact approval or transport testing.

Begin with the menu shape and the way it is served

Measure the finished flatbread at its longest and widest points after baking. Include irregular edges, raised crust, toppings that extend beyond the center and any serving paper or board used by the kitchen. Record the maximum height as well as the footprint. If the menu includes a narrow oval, a long rectangle and a shareable item with a broad center, put them in one fit matrix rather than using a single “flatbread” measurement. That makes it easier to see whether one structure can serve the whole range or whether separate lengths are more practical.

Ask how the food is cut and served. Some operators present a whole flatbread; others cut it into strips or portions before packing. The box may travel closed to a customer, open on a table or sit in a counter display. Those uses can change the desired opening side, lid stiffness, label position and whether a paper liner or serving board is part of the order. Identify which surfaces should remain untouched and how the customer is expected to lift or share the product. A useful structure follows the real use sequence instead of assuming every flatbread is handled like a round pizza.

Specify internal length, width and end clearance

For a long carton, buyers should provide internal length and width separately and identify the usable depth below the lid. The board thickness, folded wall and closure geometry reduce the clear space compared with the outside dimensions. The longest point may sit diagonally if the product is not perfectly straight, so test the actual item in the sample rather than relying on a top-view diagram. Check both ends, the corners and the narrowest section of the box. Confirm that food can be loaded without bending or sliding under a flap.

Do not size only to the nominal recipe dimension. Measure the maximum and minimum finished items from normal production. A loose fit may allow movement in a carrier; a tight fit may make loading difficult or damage the edge. Sample with any tray, separator or liner the operator expects to use. If several lengths are planned, review whether their cartons can be visually coded and stored without confusion. A dimensional drawing should state whether values refer to the clear inside, outside or blank, and should identify the tolerance agreed for the order.

Use a long format to improve service and pack-out

The rectangular footprint may help an operator keep an elongated menu item aligned with the serving tray or courier bag. Confirm this with the actual downstream equipment. Measure the inside of the insulated carrier, the space available on the pass, and the route used to stack ready orders. A box that fits the oven tray may not fit the customer’s carrier, and a long shape can take more space across a crowded counter. If the package travels with drinks or side dishes, verify the complete bag rather than the carton alone.

Opening and portion removal deserve a separate trial. Have staff open the box from the expected side and reach the item without pressing the lid into a topping. If the product is cut, observe whether pieces remain arranged during transport and whether customers can remove one portion without collapsing the others. A paper divider or support board may be considered, but it is not shown as an included component in the current images. Add accessories only when the workflow needs them and when their material, dimensions and contact status are included in the approval.

Choose a material direction based on product and route

The visual surface and color in the mockup do not identify a precise paper grade, flute, coating, recycled content or performance level. Explain the product temperature, grease exposure, expected hold time, humidity, direct or indirect food contact, and delivery destination. Request a written material description and relevant documentation for the intended use. A long span can behave differently from a short panel under load, so structural trials should include realistic filled boxes, the actual stack arrangement and the way the carton is carried.

If a barrier, liner or coating is required, state the operating condition that makes it necessary and how acceptance will be judged. The choice may influence surface print, fold quality, recycling instructions and sourcing. Review paper, ink, adhesive, barrier and accessory information together where they are relevant to the use. “Paper,” “kraft” and “food box” are descriptions, not proof of suitability for every recipe or jurisdiction. Keep long-panel artwork aligned to the dieline

A long lid gives the designer a strong horizontal canvas, but the usable print area is interrupted by scores, edges, locks and glue locations. The concept artwork contains placeholder “CUSTOM LOGO” text. Replace it with approved artwork positioned on the production dieline, not an approximate rectangle. Identify the front and serving end, the intended reading direction, the primary logo area and any panels reserved for product names or labels. Show print, trim, crease, bleed, safe zones and non-printing details on separate layers for review.

Long artwork may include a centered logo, a repeated pattern, a menu identifier or a campaign panel. Choose a hierarchy that still reads when the box is rotated or stacked. Keep small text and codes away from folds and front locks. Test the artwork at actual scale on the selected stock, because a screen preview does not establish contrast or color on the final surface. If several menu lengths use a shared design, specify what must move or resize on each dieline. Approve a physical proof or sample before production, especially where the logo must align across a crease or remain clear near a cut edge.

Check stiffness, carrying direction and stacking

A rectangular carton may be lifted from its short end, its long edge or both hands, depending on size and service. Confirm the intended carrying points and ask users to handle a filled sample. Watch for flex along the longest wall, corner opening, a lid that bows, or a product that slides toward one end. If boxes are stacked, define the normal loaded height and check the bottom carton after a representative holding period. Humidity, box orientation, product weight and cutouts can all change behavior, so avoid using a generic “stackable” claim without a stated test and documented result.

Test in the same tray, carrier and dispatch area used by the buyer. A box that stays stable on a level counter may shift when placed at an angle in a courier bag. Mark the approved orientation if staff need to keep the long side aligned. If a catering customer will open the carton horizontally on a table, confirm the lid clears the surrounding space and does not fall into the food. These practical checks help decide whether the current shallow geometry needs a different depth, closure or accessory.

Prepare a flatbread-box RFQ and common questions

The listed minimum order quantity is 1,000 boxes. A useful request includes the longest and widest finished item, maximum height, weight, photographs or physical samples, menu variation, serving method, stack plan, artwork, market, order split and target date. Include the inner size you need if already known, but ask the supplier to confirm clear dimensions on the dieline. State whether a liner or tray is desired and provide the requirements for any direct food contact. Quote scope depends on board, print area, finish, accessories, packing and delivery. Why choose a rectangular format for flatbread?

It can follow an elongated product footprint and use the lid as a horizontal brand panel. The actual benefit depends on measured fit, handling and storage compared with the buyer’s current format.

Can a standard round pizza box be used instead?

That depends on the product geometry and delivery setup. Compare clear space, movement, wasted footprint and ease of serving using filled samples in the intended carrier.

Are a liner or tray included?

The supplied concept images do not define an included accessory. Specify whether one is required, its dimensions and material, and whether it contacts food.

Do the images prove a particular box size?

No. Confirm internal length, width, depth and tolerance from the technical drawing, then trial the sample with the real product and any accessory.

What is the minimum order quantity?

The product listing uses 1,000 boxes as the minimum. Confirm quantity by size and artwork version in the written quotation.
