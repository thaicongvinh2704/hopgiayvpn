As of August 25, 2026, New York's Packaging Reduction and Recycling Infrastructure Act is not an enacted law. The current amended proposal, S.1464A / A.1749A, remains listed in the New York Senate Rules Committee after the 2026 legislative session ended without final passage. That status matters. Brands should not treat PRRIA's proposed reduction percentages, producer fees, recyclability criteria, or chemical restrictions as current compliance deadlines. But the bill is still useful as a signal of where packaging regulation is moving: away from judging a package only by what material appears on the outside and toward measuring material use, package design, recyclability in real systems, end markets, and producer responsibility. For companies using folding cartons, rigid boxes, corrugated shipping boxes, paper sleeves, or other paper-based packaging, the most important lesson is therefore not simply “switch to paper.” It is to build packaging specifications that can be verified. This article is for packaging planning and general information, not legal advice. Legislative language and implementation dates may change in a future version.

First, treat PRRIA as a proposal—not a current New York compliance rule

The Packaging Reduction and Recycling Infrastructure Act is New York's proposed extended producer responsibility, or EPR, framework for packaging. The 2026 amended bill would establish a producer-funded system covering packaging reduction, recycling infrastructure, reuse, recyclability, certain toxic substances, recycled content, and program fees. The bill moved substantially during the 2025–2026 legislative cycle. An earlier version passed the Senate in 2025, died in the Assembly, returned in 2026 as an amended version, and was committed to the Senate Rules Committee on June 5, 2026. It did not receive final passage in 2026. For a brand planning packaging today, that leads to two separate tasks:
  • Current compliance: follow laws and rules that are already applicable to the product and market.
  • Future-readiness: understand which packaging data and design decisions would become important if PRRIA, or a similar EPR framework, returns in a future legislative session.
Mixing these two tasks creates unnecessary risk. A proposed bill should not be presented internally as if it already created a legal obligation, while ignoring its direction completely may leave procurement teams without the data they would need if EPR rules later apply.

What the 2026 PRRIA proposal would ask brands to prove

The bill is broad enough that paper packaging cannot be treated as automatically outside its scope. Its definition of covered packaging includes paper, cardboard, corrugated cardboard, plastic, glass, metal, and multi-material packaging used to contain, protect, handle, deliver, transport, distribute, or present products. In other words, “we use paper instead of plastic” would not by itself answer the EPR question.
Question What the 2026 proposal says What a paper-packaging buyer should verify
Who is the producer? The bill uses a hierarchy that can place responsibility on a product manufacturer, licensee, brand owner, importer of record, shipper, or another party depending on how the product reaches New York. Do not assume the company that manufactures the empty paper box is automatically the regulated producer. Map the product, brand ownership, importer, sales channel, and contractual responsibility.
Is the business de minimis? Under the amended text, a producer is de minimis if, in its most recent fiscal year, it introduced less than two tons of covered material into New York, earned less than $5 million in global gross revenue, or meets the specified agricultural-cooperative exemption. Keep New York packaging-weight data separate from total company packaging data and document the entity whose revenue is being evaluated.
Is paper covered? Yes. Paper, cardboard and corrugated cardboard are expressly included as covered material categories, regardless of recyclability. Record the actual material category and unit weight instead of relying on a generic description such as “sustainable paper packaging.”
Would packaging have to be reduced? The amended reduction section would start at 10% and progress to 30%, measured against a producer's first registration-year baseline. The wording covers plastic packaging, including plastic primary packaging, plus non-primary secondary and tertiary packaging of all materials. Identify whether each paper box is primary, secondary, or tertiary packaging before assuming how the reduction provision would apply.
Is there a recycled-content requirement for paper boxes? The explicit paper requirement in the current amended text applies to paper carryout bags: 40% post-consumer recycled content on average, or 20% for certain smaller bags. The text does not impose that same percentage on every paper box. Do not turn a paper-bag threshold into a “40% recycled paper box” legal requirement. Specify recycled content only when supported by the actual material specification.
What would count as recyclable? The proposal would have New York evaluate collection access, sorting infrastructure, processing capability, responsible end markets, contamination and other factors. Later recyclability criteria also address features such as disruptive labels, inks and adhesives. Evaluate the finished package as a system—not only the base sheet of paperboard.
Could packaging design influence fees? Proposed producer fees would vary by material type, quantity and unit weight, with eco-modulation intended to reward reduction, recycling compatibility, recycled content and other preferred characteristics. Maintain component weights and material data so alternative structures can be compared rather than redesigned from assumptions.

For paper boxes, primary versus secondary packaging is not a minor definition

One of the easiest ways to misread PRRIA is to repeat the headline that producers would have to “reduce packaging by 30%” without checking what packaging the amended reduction provision actually describes. The 2026 text defines primary packaging as packaging in direct contact with the product. Its reduction section then refers to plastic packaging, including plastic primary packaging, and to all other non-primary packaging, including secondary and tertiary packaging of all materials. That distinction can materially change the analysis for a paper box. Consider a glass cosmetic jar placed inside a printed folding carton and then packed into a corrugated master carton. The product-contact container, the retail carton and the shipping carton perform different packaging functions. A procurement file that records all three simply as “paper/plastic packaging” loses information that could matter under an EPR framework. A better bill of materials records, for every packaging layer:
  • packaging function: primary, secondary or tertiary;
  • material family;
  • unit weight;
  • coatings, laminates and surface treatments;
  • labels and adhesive systems;
  • windows, magnets, foam, plastic trays or other separate components;
  • whether components can be separated in normal use;
  • recycled-content percentage where specified and documented.
This information is useful even if PRRIA never becomes law. It allows a brand to compare packaging designs by measurable changes instead of replacing one vague environmental claim with another.

The three-layer test: a recyclable claim is not the same as recyclable packaging

This is the most important distinction for brands buying paper packaging. A package can look environmentally preferable, contain a recyclable fiber substrate and still require additional evidence before a broad “recyclable” claim is appropriate. Three different questions have to be separated.

Layer 1: What is the brand actually claiming?

“Paper,” “made with recycled content,” “recyclable,” “plastic-free,” and “eco-friendly” are not interchangeable statements. Current Federal Trade Commission Green Guides caution against broad, unqualified environmental benefit claims that cannot be adequately substantiated. For recyclable claims, the FTC's current guidance also considers whether suitable recycling facilities are available to consumers or communities where the item is sold; its summary uses 60% availability as the threshold for an unqualified recyclable claim. That is a marketing-claim question.

Layer 2: What is the finished package actually made from?

A specification such as “350 gsm paperboard” only describes part of the finished package. A retail box can also contain film lamination, coatings, foil, labels, window film, adhesives, a plastic insert, foam, magnets or other components. None of those features should automatically be declared incompatible with recycling without evidence, but neither should they be ignored simply because the main substrate is paper. The evidence should describe the finished package configuration, not just the paper grade purchased by the converter.

Layer 3: Can that finished package move through a real recovery system?

The PRRIA proposal goes beyond theoretical material recyclability. Its draft criteria look at actual collection services, sorting technology, processing infrastructure, contamination and the existence of responsible end markets. The proposed text also specifically recognizes that some label constructions, adhesives, inks, materials or formats may disrupt recycling. This creates an important procurement rule: Do not approve “recyclable” from a substrate name alone. Match the environmental claim to the finished package and to the recovery pathway relevant to the market where the package will be sold.

What PRRIA could mean for custom paper-box specifications

For paper packaging buyers, the practical impact of an EPR framework is less about choosing one universally “green” box and more about making packaging decisions traceable.

1. Package weight becomes a controlled specification

If reduction and producer fees depend partly on packaging weight, the weight of each packaging layer stops being only a freight or costing variable. Brands need a reproducible baseline. That does not mean making every carton as thin as possible. Lightweighting that produces crushed cartons, damaged products or additional protective packaging can simply move the problem elsewhere. A useful reduction project therefore compares:
  • current unit weight;
  • revised unit weight;
  • product-protection performance;
  • shipping configuration;
  • material changes;
  • components removed or simplified.

2. Multi-material construction deserves an explicit review

The amended bill identifies paper, cardboard, corrugated cardboard and mixed materials—including laminates—as separate fee categories. A premium rigid box with paper wrap, greyboard, magnets, foam and decorative components therefore provides a different evidence problem from a simple folding carton made primarily from one paperboard grade. The correct response is not to declare one structure “bad.” It is to ask whether every component is functionally necessary, how the finished package is expected to be handled at end of life, and whether a simpler structure could provide the same protection and presentation.

3. Inks, coatings and adhesives become part of environmental documentation

The proposal contains restrictions on intentionally added substances including PFAS, specified bisphenols, heavy metals, formaldehyde, PVC/PVDC and other listed substances. It also connects recyclability and eco-modulation with packaging features that may disrupt recycling. For a paper box, a responsible sourcing file should therefore go beyond a statement such as “made of recyclable paper.” Where relevant, buyers should request specifications or declarations covering inks, coatings, adhesives, films and other added materials.

4. Recycled content needs a number and a scope

“Uses recycled material” is incomplete when the buyer cannot tell which component contains recycled material or what percentage is being claimed. For example, if recycled fiber is specified for the board but a package also contains other components, procurement records should make clear whether a percentage refers to the board, one component or the complete package. This is also why the proposed 40% recycled-content requirement for qualifying paper carryout bags should not be copied into specifications for paper boxes. They are different package types under the current bill language.

Build a packaging evidence file before redesigning the box

A brand does not need to wait for PRRIA to become law to improve its packaging data. The useful first step is an evidence file for each important packaging SKU. For a custom paper box, that file can contain:
  1. Structure drawing or dieline: showing the actual packaging construction.
  2. Packaging-layer classification: primary, secondary or tertiary based on the product configuration.
  3. Material bill of materials: board, paper wrap, corrugated material, inserts, films, windows and other components.
  4. Unit weight: measured for the finished packaging, with component weights where useful.
  5. Recycled-content documentation: percentage, material scope and supporting supplier information where a claim will be made.
  6. Printing and finishing specification: ink system, coating, lamination, foil, adhesive and special components where relevant.
  7. Recyclability evidence: information appropriate to the finished package and the intended market, rather than a statement based only on paper content.
  8. Approved environmental wording: the exact consumer-facing claim and the evidence supporting it.
This turns sustainability from a design adjective into a specification-management process. It also makes future EPR work easier. If a rule later requires reporting by material category and weight, procurement does not have to reconstruct years of packaging information from invoices that only say “printed box.”

Five conclusions brands should not draw from the current proposal

  • “PRRIA is already New York law.” It is not. S.1464A / A.1749A did not receive final passage in the 2026 session.
  • “Paper packaging is exempt.” It is not exempt simply because it is paper; paper, cardboard and corrugated cardboard are expressly included in the proposal's covered material definition.
  • “Every paper box would need 40% recycled content.” The explicit 40% provision in the amended text concerns paper carryout bags, with a lower percentage for certain smaller bags. Other material requirements could later be established under the proposal.
  • “A paper box is automatically recyclable.” The proposed framework evaluates collection, sorting, processing, contamination and responsible end markets, while FTC marketing guidance separately requires recyclable claims to be properly substantiated.
  • “The box factory is always the producer responsible for EPR.” Producer assignment depends on the product, brand, importer, sales channel and other conditions defined in the legislation.

What brands can do now without treating a proposal as law

The strongest preparation is not a rushed packaging redesign. It is better packaging information. For each major SKU sold into the United States, brands can start by identifying packaging layers, recording finished-package weight, documenting materials and finishes, separating environmental claims from technical specifications, and retaining evidence for recycled-content or recyclability statements. When considering a redesign, use a practical hierarchy: eliminate components that do not provide value, optimize dimensions, reduce material only after protection testing, simplify unnecessary material combinations, and then evaluate how printing and finishing choices affect the finished package. A lighter package that causes product damage is not a successful packaging reduction. For brands sourcing paper packaging internationally, the manufacturer can support the technical side of that process but should not be asked to make the brand's legal determination. VPN Paper Box can discuss board choices, box structures, printing requirements, dielines and samples as part of a custom project. Buyers evaluating a new specification can work with a custom packaging box manufacturer to compare manufacturable structures while keeping regulatory and environmental claims tied to evidence appropriate to the destination market. The useful takeaway from PRRIA is not “paper wins.” It is that the package, the claim and the recycling system must be evaluated separately.
