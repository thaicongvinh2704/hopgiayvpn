Paper bags do not come directly from a tree and leave the same factory as finished shopping bags. Their journey normally crosses two separate production environments: a paper mill that turns cellulose fibre into a paper roll or sheet, and a converting plant that prints, cuts, folds, glues and finishes that paper into a functional bag. The fibre may come from fresh wood-based pulp, recovered paper or a controlled mixture of both. From there, the selected paper grade, surface, grammage and fibre composition influence how the bag prints, folds, carries weight and responds to moisture. That distinction matters for B2B packaging buyers. Asking only for a “100 GSM paper bag” does not fully describe the product. Two papers with the same grammage can behave differently because their fibre length, recycled content, surface treatment, grain direction and converting quality are not identical.

A Paper Bag Usually Comes From Two Factories, Not One

The simplest accurate answer to “where do paper bags come from?” is that they come from a supply chain rather than a single machine. The first part of that supply chain is the paper mill. The mill receives wood-based raw material, market pulp or recovered paper and converts it into a controlled paper grade. The output is usually a large reel or a stack of sheets, not a finished bag. The second part is the paper bag converter. This factory turns the paper into a three-dimensional packaging structure. Depending on the design, the converter may print the paper, form side gussets, create the bottom, glue the longitudinal seam, attach handles and pack the finished bags for shipment. Some vertically integrated manufacturers control more than one stage, but buyers should still treat papermaking and bag converting as two different technical processes. A converter cannot completely correct weak or unsuitable base paper, while good base paper can still become a poor bag if the folding, gluing or handle attachment is inaccurate.

The First Origin: Cellulose Fibre

Most commercial paper bags begin with cellulose fibres. These fibres are separated, cleaned and prepared before they are formed into paper. The source and condition of the fibres influence tear resistance, stiffness, surface appearance and the consistency of the final bag.

Fresh fibre for strength and predictable converting

Fresh-fibre kraft paper is commonly produced from wood-based pulp. Long fibres are particularly useful when a paper grade needs good tensile and tear performance. The natural brown colour associated with many kraft bags usually comes from unbleached pulp, while white kraft requires additional processing to produce a lighter printing surface. Fresh fibre does not automatically mean that every finished bag is stronger. Bag dimensions, paper formulation, grain direction, handle reinforcement, bottom construction and exposure to humidity also affect performance. However, fibre quality provides the starting strength that later converting stages must preserve.

Recovered paper for recycled-content grades

Recovered paper can also be repulped and used to manufacture paper for bags. The incoming material must be sorted, broken down in water and cleaned to remove contaminants that are not useful in papermaking. Depending on the grade, the mill may combine recycled fibres with fresh fibres to balance appearance, strength, cost and recycled content. Recycled kraft paper can be suitable for many retail and promotional applications, but “recycled” is not a complete technical specification. Buyers should confirm the actual paper grade, recycled-content claim, test data and expected load rather than assuming that all recycled papers perform in the same way.

How Fibre Becomes a Paper Roll

Before a bag can be printed or folded, loose fibres must become a continuous and consistent sheet. The exact mill configuration differs by paper grade, but the route normally follows several connected stages.
  1. Raw-material preparation: Wood is prepared for pulping, or recovered paper is sorted and repulped.
  2. Pulping and cleaning: Fibres are separated from unwanted material and cleaned to create usable pulp.
  3. Stock preparation: Fibre composition and permitted additives are adjusted for the target strength, formation, colour and surface properties.
  4. Sheet formation: Diluted pulp is distributed across a moving forming section so water can drain and fibres can create a continuous web.
  5. Pressing and drying: Additional water is removed before the paper passes through a controlled drying section.
  6. Surface and reel preparation: The paper may be calendered, glazed, coated, coloured or otherwise finished before being wound into reels or cut into sheets.
The converter receives this paper as a production material. At that point, the paper already has a defined grammage, moisture level, fibre direction, surface character and mechanical profile. Those properties affect the machine settings and bag designs that can be used later.

GSM Tells You Weight, Not the Whole Performance Story

GSM means grams per square metre. It describes the mass of the paper over a defined area. It does not directly state how much weight the finished bag can carry. A higher GSM often creates a heavier and potentially stiffer sheet, but performance also depends on fibre composition, tensile strength, tear resistance, paper formation, moisture, grain direction and bag geometry. This is why reducing GSM does not always reduce bag strength in direct proportion, and increasing GSM does not automatically correct a weak seam or badly attached handle. For procurement, GSM should be recorded alongside the exact paper grade or supplier reference. For higher-risk projects, the specification should also define the finished bag dimensions, handle construction, reinforcement method and an agreed loading or carrying test.

Printing Starts Before the Bag Has Its Final Shape

Most branded paper bags are printed while the paper is still flat. The correct printing route depends on whether the production begins with a reel or individual sheets, how complex the artwork is and what type of surface the buyer expects. Flexographic printing is commonly associated with reel-fed production. It works well for high-volume paper bags, simple logos, repeated graphics and designs that can be converted efficiently in line or through connected production stages. Offset printing is often used when premium sheet-fed bags require detailed images, tighter colour control or a smooth coated surface. After printing and finishing, the sheets are die-cut, folded and assembled into bags. Digital printing can support samples, prototypes, variable designs or selected shorter production runs. Its suitability depends on the printing equipment, paper surface, bag dimensions and later converting route. Foil stamping, embossing, debossing and lamination are separate finishing decisions rather than substitutes for the main printing method. They can improve presentation, but every added layer or component should be assessed for folding behaviour, cracking risk and compatibility with the intended recovery stream.

The Second Origin: Where Flat Paper Becomes a Bag

Once the paper and artwork route are approved, the converter transforms the flat material into a usable structure. For a typical reel-fed square-bottom bag, several operations may happen within one connected production line.
  1. Reel unwinding: The paper roll is fed into the machine under controlled tension.
  2. Printing: Artwork may be applied before the paper reaches the main forming section.
  3. Tube and gusset formation: The flat web is folded into a tube, while side gussets create expandable volume.
  4. Longitudinal gluing: An overlapping side seam closes the tube.
  5. Cutting: The continuous tube is separated into individual bag lengths.
  6. Bottom formation: The base is opened, folded and glued into the required flat or square-bottom structure.
  7. Handle application: Flat paper, twisted paper, rope or ribbon handles may be attached, depending on the bag design.
  8. Inspection and packing: Finished bags are counted, checked and packed into outer cartons.
Sheet-fed premium bags follow a different route. Printed sheets may be laminated or finished, die-cut to shape and then folded and glued through automated, semi-automated or manual assembly stages. This route allows more decorative materials and finishes, but it also introduces additional alignment and workmanship controls.

The Bag’s Weakest Point Is Often Created After Papermaking

A paper mill controls the base sheet, but many visible bag failures originate during converting. The paper can be technically suitable while the finished bag still fails because the handle patch is too small, glue coverage is inconsistent, the bottom fold is inaccurate or the bag dimensions create excessive stress at one point. This article does not repeat the full strength-engineering discussion covered in our guide on how to make paper bags stronger. The important connection is that material and converting should be evaluated together. Paper origin explains the strength potential; construction determines how efficiently the finished bag uses that potential.

A Brief Historical Turning Point

Paper bags existed before modern automated converting, but early bags were closer to flat envelopes. The development of machinery capable of folding and gluing flat-bottom bags made them more practical for holding and carrying goods. Margaret E. Knight’s nineteenth-century work on a machine for producing flat-bottom paper bags became an important milestone in that transition. Modern production lines retain the same basic structural challenge: turn a flat paper web into a bag that can open, stand, carry and close consistently. The equipment is faster and more controlled, but the logic of folds, seams and bottom formation remains central.

Where Paper Bags Fit in a B2B Packaging System

A paper bag is usually the outer carrying and presentation layer. It should not be expected to perform every job of the packaging system. A folding carton or rigid box may provide product protection, stacking strength, information panels and the main unboxing experience. A paper bag then helps the customer carry that box while extending the same visual identity beyond the store or event. For corporate gifts, cosmetics, jewellery, fashion and premium food products, buyers often coordinate the bag with a matching box, tissue paper, paper insert, sleeve, tag or card. The materials do not need to be identical, but colour, texture, size and finishing should look intentional as a complete system. Businesses developing both formats can use a custom packaging box manufacturer to review how the bag relates to the inner box rather than sourcing each item without shared dimensions. The bag should provide enough clearance for the box to enter and leave without damaging the handles, corners or printed surfaces. For example, a premium rigid box may require a structured paper bag with a wider gusset and reinforced handles. A lightweight folding carton may only need a standard kraft carrier. Small paper accessories can then complete the system without making the bag unnecessarily heavy or complex.

Use This Short Procurement Brief Before Requesting a Paper Bag

Instead of requesting only a bag size and GSM, prepare the following project information:
  • Product type and total packed weight
  • Dimensions of the product or inner paper box
  • Required finished bag width, gusset and height
  • Preferred paper appearance: natural brown, white, coloured, coated or textured
  • Fresh-fibre, recycled-content or other sourcing requirement that must be documented
  • Handle type and expected carrying conditions
  • Artwork colours, print coverage and required finishing
  • Whether the bag will be used for food, retail, gifting, shipping or an event
  • Destination market and any humidity or storage concerns
  • Quantity, packing method and whether a physical sample is required
The most useful decision rule is simple: specify the finished job before selecting the paper. A bag comes from fibres, but its success comes from matching the fibre, paper grade, printing route and converting structure to one clearly defined application. When requesting a quotation, send the product or box dimensions, packed weight, preferred handle, artwork and destination market. Those details give the packaging supplier enough information to recommend a paper and bag structure that can be evaluated through a physical sample before bulk production.
