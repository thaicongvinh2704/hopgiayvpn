A kraft paper bag with handles should not be specified by paper weight alone. A bag may use relatively heavy kraft paper and still fail if the handle attachment, top reinforcement, bottom construction or gusset is poorly matched to the packed load. For B2B buyers, the useful specification is therefore a complete carrying system: paper grade, GSM, bag dimensions, handle type, reinforcement, artwork and the actual products the bag must carry. The practical goal is not to choose the highest GSM available. It is to choose a construction that carries the required products reliably, prints the artwork acceptably and avoids unnecessary material or cost. This guide compares the main kraft paper bag handle systems, typical GSM starting points and custom-printing considerations before a buyer approves a production sample.

Start With the Load Path, Not the GSM Number

When a customer lifts a handled paper bag, the product weight does not act on the paper uniformly. The load moves from the bag bottom through the side panels and gussets toward a relatively small area where each handle is attached. This creates several potential failure points:
  • the bottom can bow or open;
  • the side seam can separate;
  • the top edge can deform;
  • paper can tear around handle holes or patches;
  • the handle itself can pull loose;
  • a deep gusset can lose stability under a concentrated load.
For this reason, increasing GSM may improve stiffness without solving the actual weak point. A stronger specification evaluates paper, folds, adhesive, handle attachment and reinforcement together. GSM means grams per square meter. It describes the mass of a square meter of paper, but it does not directly describe the finished bag's carrying capacity. Two kraft papers at the same GSM can differ in tensile performance, tear behavior, stiffness, surface characteristics and converting performance. Three Reinforcement Zones Determine Whether a Handled Bag Works

1. Top and handle attachment

Rope and ribbon handles apply force through small attachment points. A folded-over top, internal reinforcement patch or board strip can distribute this force across a larger area. The correct solution depends on handle type, bag size and the expected packed weight.

2. Bottom construction

The bottom carries the vertical load before that force reaches the handles. Dense products such as candles, perfume sets, glass jars or multiple rigid boxes can create a very different stress pattern from apparel of the same external size. A bottom reinforcement card may be appropriate when the product creates concentrated pressure or when the brand wants the bag to remain flatter and more structured while being carried.

3. Gussets and glued seams

Side and bottom gussets allow the bag to open, but their geometry also influences how the loaded bag changes shape. Deep bags intended for multiple products should be tested after packing rather than judged in an empty condition. The side seam and bottom glue areas are equally important. A visually premium bag is not a successful specification if the structure changes significantly after several minutes of carrying.

Custom Printing Must Be Matched to the Bag Production Route

Kraft bags can be produced through different converting routes, so the appropriate print process depends on quantity, paper grade, bag style, graphics and supplier equipment.

Flexographic printing

Flexographic printing is commonly associated with high-volume roll-fed paper bag production and works particularly well for logos, repeated artwork and controlled color layouts. Artwork complexity and color count should be discussed before plates and production specifications are approved.

Offset printing

Offset printing can be suitable when sheet-fed paper is printed before bag converting and when the design requires sharper detail or more sophisticated graphics. The planned folds, glue areas, handle holes and finishing positions must already be reflected in the artwork layout.

Digital printing

Digital production can be useful for certain shorter-run or sampling routes where tooling and production conditions permit it. Buyers should confirm that the digital sample represents the intended bulk appearance if the final production route will use another printing method.

Foil, embossing and other finishing

Premium handled bags can also combine printed artwork with treatments such as foil stamping or embossing. These finishes should not be positioned blindly across folds, handle holes or highly stressed areas. A decorative element that looks correct on the flat sheet can move, crack or become visually distorted after the bag is folded and loaded. A Sample Should Be Approved Under Load, Not While Empty

An empty kraft bag can look straight, clean and strong while hiding the conditions that will cause failure. The meaningful approval test uses the actual packed product or a weight-accurate equivalent. A practical sample review can follow this sequence:
  1. Pack the real retail combination. Include the product, primary box, inserts, tissue or accessories that the customer will actually carry.
  2. Confirm loading clearance. Staff should be able to place and remove the pack without forcing corners against the bag.
  3. Lift from both handles. Watch the paper around handle attachments as load transfers upward.
  4. Carry the bag for a realistic period. Do not judge the structure from a five-second lift.
  5. Inspect the bottom. Check bowing, fold opening, separation and concentrated pressure marks.
  6. Inspect the top and handle zone. Look for elongation of holes, patch movement, tearing or permanent deformation.
  7. Repeat normal handling. Load, carry and unload several times when reuse during the retail journey is expected.
  8. Review the printed surfaces after testing. Check rub, cracking around folds and damage caused by the packed product.
This test is more informative than selecting GSM from a generic chart because it validates the complete bag construction under the condition that matters: the intended packed load.

Build the RFQ Around the Finished Bag Specification

A supplier can recommend material and construction more reliably when the inquiry contains enough information to reproduce the real use case.
  • finished bag width, height and side/bottom gusset if already known;
  • product or finished box dimensions;
  • complete expected packed weight;
  • number of products normally placed in one bag;
  • preferred natural brown or white kraft direction;
  • preferred handle type and approximate handle drop;
  • whether a reinforced top or bottom board is required or open for recommendation;
  • artwork file and number of print colors;
  • Pantone references where color matching matters;
  • special finishing such as foil, embossing or lamination if required;
  • order quantity by size or artwork version;
  • destination market and required packing format;
  • sample and functional approval requirements.
Projects that combine carriers with tissue, protective components or coordinated box-and-bag sets can also review available packaging accessories before the complete retail pack is finalized.
