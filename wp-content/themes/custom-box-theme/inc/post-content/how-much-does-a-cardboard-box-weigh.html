An empty cardboard box can weigh from under 50 g (about 2 oz) for a small microflute mailer to more than 1 kg (2.2 lb) for a large or double-wall shipping case. A typical medium single-wall corrugated shipping box often falls somewhere around 300–700 g (11–25 oz), but dimensions alone cannot give an exact answer. Board construction, flute, box style, inserts, coatings and moisture all change the result. If you only need a rough planning figure, use the comparison table below. If the weight will affect a shipping quote, packaging specification or large production order, estimate it from the flat box blank and confirm it by weighing a finished sample.

Three different “weights” can appear in one shipping discussion

Many inaccurate answers start by using the right numbers for the wrong purpose. Before calculating anything, identify which weight you need:
  • Empty box weight, or tare weight: the mass of the unfilled box itself.
  • Actual packed weight, or gross weight: the box plus product, insert, filler, literature, tape and labels.
  • Dimensional weight: a carrier pricing value based on the space occupied by the packed parcel. It is not the physical weight of the cardboard.
For packaging procurement, the empty weight helps compare constructions and estimate material use. For freight booking, the gross actual weight is normally compared with dimensional weight under the selected carrier’s current rules. The billable value may be the greater of the two.

Why L × W × H ÷ 139 cannot tell you what the box weighs

A formula such as length × width × height ÷ a carrier divisor produces dimensional weight. It answers: “How much shipping space does this parcel consume?” It does not account for liner GSM, corrugated medium, flute profile, flaps or glue joints. A thin single-wall box and a heavy double-wall box with identical outside dimensions therefore produce the same dimensional weight even though their physical weights differ. This distinction matters because online guides sometimes present a dimensional-weight result as the weight of the empty box. For example, a 12 × 12 × 12 in parcel divided by 166 produces about 10.4 lb. A normal empty corrugated box of that size does not physically weigh 10.4 lb; the calculation is a volume-based rating figure using that divisor. Decision rule: use a scale or a board-area calculation for empty box weight. Use the carrier’s current dimensional-weight method only for freight rating.

The practical formula uses the flat blank, not the box volume

For a corrugated box, the most useful production estimate is: Estimated empty box weight (g) = die-cut blank area (m²) × combined corrugated board grammage (g/m²) The blank area is the area of the flat piece after its panel layout has been defined. It includes body panels, top and bottom flaps, locking wings and the manufacturer’s joint. It is not simply the surface area of a perfect six-sided cuboid. A mailer may have doubled side walls and several locking tabs, so its blank can use substantially more board than its assembled footprint suggests. The combined board grammage is the mass per square metre of the finished corrugated board. It includes the liners and the fluted medium. When a supplier provides this finished-board value, the flute take-up has already been represented; do not multiply the number again by an arbitrary “wall factor.” For a preliminary estimate built from individual paper specifications, the corrugated medium requires a take-up allowance because the fluted paper path is longer than a flat sheet. Ask the board supplier for the confirmed combined GSM rather than guessing that allowance.

Worked example: a regular slotted shipping box

Consider an illustrative RSC with internal dimensions of 300 × 200 × 150 mm. Assume a 40 mm manufacturer’s joint and an approximate blank layout of:
  • Blank length: 2 × 300 + 2 × 200 + 40 = 1,040 mm
  • Blank width: 150 + 200 = 350 mm
  • Approximate blank area: 1.04 × 0.35 = 0.364 m²
If the specified combined board is 500 g/m², the estimated empty-box weight is: 0.364 m² × 500 g/m² = 182 g (about 6.4 oz) If a different construction raises the combined board grammage to 650 g/m², the same approximate blank becomes 237 g (about 8.4 oz). This is why “What are the box dimensions?” is not enough. The board specification must accompany the dimensions. This example is a planning calculation, not a cutting file. Corrugator allowances, slot depth, score positions, joint design and the distinction between internal and external dimensions should be confirmed on the actual dieline. The guide to paper box dielines explains why the flat layout is the reliable starting point for material-area calculations.

A mailer needs its CAD blank area—and its insert counted separately

Corrugated mailers are harder to estimate from assembled length, width and height. Roll-end sides, dust flaps, front locks and doubled walls all add material. The cleanest method is to obtain the net area from the approved CAD dieline. Suppose an illustrative mailer dieline has a net blank area of 0.62 m² and uses 480 g/m² combined corrugated board: Mailer: 0.62 × 480 = 298 g Now suppose its corrugated insert uses another 0.18 m² of 420 g/m² board: Insert: 0.18 × 420 = 76 g The corrugated packaging subtotal is therefore about 374 g (13.2 oz) before labels, tape, literature or other components are added. Leaving the insert out of the estimate would understate packaging mass by roughly one fifth in this particular example. This insert effect is easy to miss when quotes describe only the outer box. For a fair supplier comparison, every quote should state whether its unit weight includes the insert, divider, sleeve or internal pad.

What changes the weight even when the outside size stays fixed?

Board construction and paper grammage

Single-wall corrugated board contains two liners and one fluted medium. Double-wall contains additional paper layers. However, “double-wall is heavier” is not a complete specification: the GSM of each component, flute combination and board performance requirement still matter. Box weight is not a substitute for compression, puncture or edge-crush performance.

Flute and box style

Flute affects board thickness, cushioning, print surface and paper consumption. Box style affects the amount of overlap. A compact mailer with reinforced roll-over edges may use more board than a simple RSC enclosing a similar volume. Compare the actual blank areas, not just the assembled sizes.

Inserts, dividers and doubled panels

A protective insert can add meaningful weight, especially in small e-commerce packs. It may still reduce total shipment material if it replaces loose filler or allows a smaller outer box, but that trade-off should be evaluated as a complete pack rather than one component at a time.

Printing, lamination, coating and adhesives

Ink and adhesive usually contribute less mass than the corrugated board, but laminated liners, barrier treatments and large coated areas can make the difference measurable across a high-volume order. Request a finished sample when unit weight is tied to a strict logistics threshold.

Moisture and measurement conditions

Paper-based packaging exchanges moisture with its environment. Samples stored under different humidity conditions may not return identical scale readings. If unit-weight consistency is a QC requirement, define when and under what storage conditions samples will be measured.

When accuracy matters, weigh a production-representative sample

A calculation is valuable before a dieline is finalized. A physical scale is better after the finished construction exists. Use a sample carrying the intended board, print, coating, glue pattern and insert—not an unprinted structural mock-up made from substitute material.
  1. Tare the scale and confirm that its resolution suits the box size.
  2. Weigh the complete empty packaging set, clearly defining which components are included.
  3. Check several units rather than treating one unit as the whole production result.
  4. Record the average and observed range for internal planning.
  5. Weigh the fully packed pack separately for shipping data.
For a large production order, the buyer and supplier should agree on a sampling and acceptance method appropriate to the project. A target such as “under 500 g per empty pack” is incomplete unless the included components, measurement condition and acceptable variation are also clear.

Reduce packaging weight without quietly reducing protection

Right-size the internal cavity Less board area and lower dimensional weight Loading clearance, product movement and packing speed

The lightest box is not automatically the lowest-cost pack. If a lighter construction needs more filler, causes damage or requires a larger master carton, total system cost can rise. Select corrugated board around product weight, fragility, stacking, fulfillment and transit conditions. Send these eight inputs to receive a usable unit-weight estimate

Before requesting a custom box quotation, prepare:
  1. Box style or a reference structure
  2. Internal dimensions, with units clearly stated
  3. Product dimensions and packed product weight
  4. Preferred corrugated construction or required performance
  5. Flute direction if it is structurally important
  6. Insert, divider, pad and literature requirements
  7. Printing, lamination or coating requirements
  8. Shipping route, parcel method and master-carton arrangement
Ask the supplier to return the proposed combined board specification, CAD blank area, estimated empty unit weight and the components included in that figure. Then confirm the result on a finished sample. If you are developing custom corrugated mailer boxes, send the product size, packed weight, preferred mailer style, insert requirement and shipping method so the structure and unit-weight estimate can be reviewed together.
