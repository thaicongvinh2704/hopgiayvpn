Printing and finishing need a production-aware approval path

Printing is not only a decoration decision. Substrate color, surface texture, ink coverage, lamination and the position of folds can change the final appearance. A buyer should share the artwork status, color references and viewing conditions before selecting the print route.

  • Offset printing: useful for repeatable color and larger production runs when the artwork and quantity justify the setup.
  • Digital printing: useful for short runs, prototypes or projects that need a quicker route to a physical reference.
  • Pantone and CMYK notes: define the intended color reference, while a physical proof confirms how the chosen stock actually behaves.
  • Lamination: matte, gloss or soft-touch finishes change glare, surface feel and scuff visibility.
  • Foil, embossing, debossing and spot UV: require clear artwork layers, safe areas and a review of how the effect sits on the selected structure.
  • Die-cutting and inserts: must be checked with the finished product, not only with an empty flat dieline.

The right question is not whether a supplier lists many finishes. Ask which finish is appropriate for the product, surface, budget, quantity and handling route, and then approve it on a relevant sample.

Sampling is the gate between a promising quote and a safe order

A supplier shortlist becomes more useful when the buyer defines what the sample must prove. The sample should represent the intended structure and material closely enough to expose fit, print, finish, assembly and handling risks before mass production.

  1. Confirm the brief: share product dimensions, finished packaging dimensions, quantity, opening style, insert needs and delivery country.
  2. Review the dieline: check bleed, safe area, folds, glue tabs, logo placement, grain direction and any interference with the product.
  3. Approve material and finish: record board or paper direction, surface, printing method, color reference and finishing notes.
  4. Test the real product: place the product in the sample and check fit, movement, closure, display angle and removal.
  5. Run a handling check: fold, assemble, stack, pack and unpack the sample in a sequence that resembles the real operation.
  6. Record acceptance limits: sign the approved reference and define the visible defects, color tolerance and structural points that matter.

Quality control should be connected to the finished packaging

Quality checking should cover more than whether the printed logo is visible. Depending on the project, the buyer may need checks for dimensions, board or paper direction, color reference, print registration, finish coverage, die-cut accuracy, glue or assembly, insert fit, surface scuffing, quantity and carton packing.

For export orders, ask how finished packaging is counted, arranged, protected and labelled for shipment. A good export-ready packing plan is part of the specification because compression, moisture exposure, carton movement and handling can affect the condition of the goods before they reach the buyer.

These checks should be agreed before production rather than invented after a shipment arrives. If a project has a sensitive surface, high-contrast artwork, tight insert fit or premium finish, make that risk visible in the RFQ and sample acceptance note.
