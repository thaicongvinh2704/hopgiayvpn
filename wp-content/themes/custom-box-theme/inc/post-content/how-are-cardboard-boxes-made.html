At the wet end of a corrugator, three rolls of paper are moving at the same time. One roll will become the wavy medium. The other two will remain flat as the inner and outer liners. Only after heat, forming, starch bonding, drying, cutting, printing, and converting does that continuous paper web become the box people recognize. To understand how cardboard boxes are made, start with that distinction: the factory does not simply cut a thick sheet and fold it into a cube. For a typical corrugated shipping box, production has two major phases: making corrugated board from containerboard rolls, then converting that board into a printed, cut, scored, and joined box. Folding cartons and rigid boxes follow different routes, even though consumers may call all three “cardboard boxes.”

First identify which “cardboard box” is being made

The keyword “cardboard box” covers several paper-based structures. The process below focuses on corrugated boxes because that is the dominant meaning in shipping, storage, and ecommerce. This comparison prevents the corrugator process from being incorrectly applied to a cereal carton or a rigid gift box.
Box family Main material How the structure is created Typical use
Corrugated shipping box or mailer One or more fluted mediums bonded between flat linerboards Board is made on a corrugator, then printed, slotted or die-cut, scored, folded, and joined Shipping, ecommerce, distribution, protective packaging
Folding carton Solid paperboard without a fluted middle layer Paperboard sheets are printed, finished, cut and creased with a die, then folded and glued Cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, food, small retail products
Rigid or set-up box Thick greyboard wrapped with printed or specialty paper Greyboard pieces are cut, assembled into a fixed shape, and wrapped; the box is not normally shipped flat Gift sets, jewelry, premium cosmetics, electronics
If the project uses solid paperboard rather than fluted board, the related paper box manufacturing process explains printing, finishing, die-cutting, folding, and quality control in more detail. The sections below follow the corrugated route.

Stage 1: paper mills make linerboard and corrugating medium

Corrugated production begins before the box plant. A paper mill prepares fiber from an appropriate mix of recovered paper and/or virgin pulp according to the required paper grade. The fiber is cleaned, refined, diluted in water, and formed into a continuous sheet on a paper machine. Water is removed through drainage, pressing, and heated drying before the finished paper is wound into large rolls. The box plant normally receives two functional paper grades:
  • Linerboard is engineered to remain relatively flat and form the inner and outer faces of the board. Its surface also affects print appearance, abrasion behavior, and the outside presentation of the box.
  • Corrugating medium is engineered to form into stable arches without cracking. Those arches become the flutes that separate the liners and contribute to cushioning and stacking performance.
Paper grade, basis weight, moisture, and roll condition influence later stages. A stronger-looking paper specification does not rescue a poorly bonded or crushed flute. Production planning therefore treats the paper rolls as controlled inputs, not interchangeable brown paper.

The flute is made before the box exists

The corrugator is a continuous line that turns paper rolls into flat corrugated sheets. According to FEFCO’s corrugated production overview, a corrugator brings together three, five, or seven sheets of paper to make single-, double-, or triple-wall board. A common single-wall board uses one medium between two liners.
  1. Unwinding and conditioning: linerboard and medium unwind under controlled tension. Heat and steam condition the medium so it can form without uncontrolled cracking.
  2. Flute formation at the single facer: the medium passes between heated corrugating rolls with matching grooves. The paper takes the repeating wave profile specified for the job.
  3. First bond: starch-based adhesive is applied to the exposed flute tips. One liner is pressed onto the medium, producing single-face corrugated board.
  4. Second bond at the double backer: adhesive is applied to the remaining flute tips, the second liner is added, and heat helps set the bond while the board travels through the line.
  5. Slitting, scoring, and cut-off: the continuous board is trimmed to job width, given any planned cross-direction scores, cut to sheet length, and stacked for converting.
The arches are not decorative. If the flute is crushed by excessive pressure, poor handling, or an unsuitable downstream setup, the board may lose caliper and part of the structural performance the design expected. If starch application or heat is poorly controlled, the liners may separate. Board leaving the dry end should therefore be checked before print and cutting hide or amplify the defect.

Stage 3: converting turns flat corrugated sheets into box blanks

A stack of corrugated sheets is work in process, not yet a usable box. Converting gives the board its graphics, panels, flaps, slots, locking tabs, and manufacturer’s joint. The exact route depends on the structural design.

The dieline translates dimensions into cuts and scores

The structural drawing defines panel sizes, flap depth, scores, slots, cutouts, locking features, and the glue or stitch joint. A simple regular slotted container can be produced with straight-line cutting and scoring tools. A mailer with curved tabs, hand holes, ventilation, or self-locking features normally requires rotary or flatbed die-cutting. Before artwork is finalized, the paper box dieline should match the chosen board thickness, flute direction, and converting equipment.

Printing may happen directly or through a separate printed layer

Flexographic printing is widely used for direct printing on corrugated board and can be integrated with slotting, scoring, folding, and gluing. Digital direct print can support other run lengths and graphic requirements. Higher-detail retail graphics may use a separately printed sheet laminated to corrugated material. The choice affects surface appearance, registration control, tooling, setup, and cost; it is not only a choice between “basic” and “premium” printing.

Cutting, scoring, folding, and joining complete the blank

A flexo folder-gluer can print, create straight slots and scores, fold the blank, and glue the manufacturer’s joint in one line. More complex die-cut items may leave a die cutter flat and move to a separate folder-gluer. Some heavy-duty joints are stitched rather than glued. The completed boxes are usually counted, bundled, and palletized in knocked-down-flat form so the customer can erect them during packing. The Packaging Corporation of America’s process guide makes an important plant distinction: a full-line plant has both corrugating and converting equipment, while a sheet plant converts purchased corrugated sheets. The finished result can be similar, but the material flow and production controls are not identical.

Quality is controlled at the handoffs, not only on the finished box

Waiting until the final pallet to find a defect is expensive. A practical control plan checks the output of each stage before it becomes the input to the next:
  • After corrugating: inspect board flatness, flute formation, caliper, liner-to-medium bonding, moisture balance, warp, and visible surface damage.
  • After printing: compare color and graphic position against the approved standard; inspect ink coverage, smearing, print-to-cut registration, and barcode or handling-mark readability where applicable.
  • After slotting or die-cutting: confirm panel dimensions, score position, slot depth, tab shape, cut cleanliness, and flute direction. A blank can look correct when flat but become out of square when erected.
  • After folding and joining: check the manufacturer’s joint, adhesive coverage or stitches, folding alignment, fishtailing, and whether the box opens and closes without tearing.
  • Before packing: verify count, bundle condition, pallet protection, labels, and moisture protection appropriate to storage and transport.
Performance checks depend on the product and distribution route. Dimensions, board construction, edge crush or burst requirements, box compression, drop handling, vibration, and packed-product fit may all matter, but no single test represents every shipment. The buyer and supplier should agree on the relevant acceptance criteria before mass production.

Worked example: an E-flute mailer for a skincare set

Consider an illustrative project—not a universal specification. A brand needs an ecommerce mailer for a 700 g skincare set measuring approximately 180 × 120 × 45 mm. The products need a paperboard insert, the outer box needs simple two-color graphics, and the parcel will travel through a small-parcel delivery network.
  1. Structure planning: the designer creates a self-locking mailer around the product and insert, adding working clearance for loading and closure rather than copying the product dimensions directly.
  2. Board selection: a single-wall E-flute direction is considered because the project needs a relatively compact profile and a printable outer surface. The final liner and medium grades still need to be confirmed through sample and performance review.
  3. Corrugating: the selected liners and medium are formed and bonded on the corrugator, then cut into sheets planned for the mailer’s die layout.
  4. Printing: the approved two-color artwork is printed on the outer liner with registration areas kept away from critical folds and locking tabs.
  5. Die-cutting: the printed sheets are cut and creased into mailer blanks. The paperboard insert is produced as a separate die-cut component because it follows a different material and folding path.
  6. Sample gate: an erected sample is packed with the real products. The team checks fit, movement, closure, print alignment, scuffing, tab wear, and protection under the intended handling test.
  7. Bulk conversion and packing: approved blanks and inserts are produced, counted, bundled flat, protected in master cartons, and labeled for shipment.
This example shows why “make the box 200 × 140 × 60 mm” is not a production brief. Product weight, insert geometry, loading method, graphics, flute direction, delivery handling, and acceptance testing all change how the factory plans the job.

What a factory needs before choosing the production route

To move from a box idea to a manufacturable specification, prepare:
  • Product dimensions, weight, fragility, and photos.
  • Required internal box size or the product arrangement that must fit.
  • Preferred style, such as regular slotted container, die-cut mailer, tray, or retail-ready box.
  • Order quantity and expected repeat-order pattern.
  • Shipping channel, pallet stacking, storage conditions, and delivery country.
  • Printing colors, artwork status, barcode or handling-mark requirements, and surface finish.
  • Insert, divider, window, handle, ventilation, or special closure requirements.
  • Sample and performance checks needed before bulk approval.
Send that brief to a custom packaging box manufacturer before locking the artwork. The supplier can then select the appropriate paper packaging route—corrugated, folding carton, or rigid construction—and align material, dieline, printing, converting, insert, testing, and export packing around the actual product rather than a generic box description.
