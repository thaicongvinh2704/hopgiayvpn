The phrase “custom pizza boxes with logo” sounds specific until three suppliers return three different quotations for the same 14-inch box. One may be pricing a single-wall B-flute box with a kraft liner and one-color flexographic print. Another may be assuming a white liner, offset artwork and a different food-facing construction. A third may be quoting a different size, quantity split or sample route altogether. That is why a comparable quote starts with a frozen specification. Before asking for price, define the box dimensions, board construction, printable surface, food-contact condition, artwork, quantity by SKU and sample acceptance criteria. This guide turns those decisions into one buyer-ready workflow.

Part 1: Start with the use condition, not the logo

A logo is only one layer of a pizza-box specification. First describe how the box will be used: takeaway, delivery, counter service, catering or another route. Note whether the pizza is hot when packed, whether steam will collect inside the box, how much grease is expected, how long the food may remain inside and whether the box will be stacked during delivery. If you are turning this checklist into a production brief, a custom packaging box manufacturer should be able to map the same inputs to board, printing and sample recommendations. The buyer still owns the intended-use decision; the supplier should not be expected to guess it from a product name.
Input to freeze Buyer question Why it changes the quote
Usable size What pizza diameter, height and clearance are required? A few millimetres can change the dieline, sheet yield and fit.
Food condition Is the product hot, greasy, wet, chilled or held for delivery? The inner liner, surface treatment and validation route may change.
Delivery handling Will boxes be stacked, transported flat or assembled before use? Strength, flat-pack efficiency and pack-out requirements affect cost.
Brand artwork How many print sides, colors, SKUs and artwork versions are needed? Print setup and plate or file preparation can vary by version.
Order plan What quantity is needed for each size and artwork? MOQ is often shaped by setup and SKU splits, not only total units.

Part 2: Specify the board as outer liner, flute and inner liner

“Kraft pizza box” or “B-flute pizza box” is not a complete board specification. A corrugated pizza box combines a printable outer liner, a fluted medium and a food-facing inner liner. Each layer contributes a different performance or appearance characteristic.
Layer What to define Buyer decision
Outer liner Kraft or white liner, surface, print appearance and agreed caliper or grade. Choose the visual base and the surface that can carry the approved artwork.
Flute B-flute, E-flute or another agreed profile, with the supplier's board specification. Balance stacking strength, box feel, print surface and available internal clearance.
Inner liner Food-facing paper, grease resistance, coating or treatment and intended contact condition. Confirm what touches the food and which project documents support that use.
Kraft and white are not interchangeable in the quotation. Natural kraft can change the appearance of brand colors and may suit a deliberately uncoated look. A white liner can provide a cleaner color background but may involve a different paper construction or print expectation. Ask the supplier to state the liner, flute, total board specification and print side in the quotation instead of accepting a generic “corrugated” label. Do not choose a flute from the pizza diameter alone. The box must close without crushing the product, retain enough clearance for toppings and steam, and survive the expected stacking and delivery route. For dimension, fit and B/E-flute questions, use the large pizza box dimensions guide as a separate sizing reference, then carry the selected dimensions into the RFQ.

Part 3: Match the logo printing method to the board

The print route should be selected with the liner and run plan, not after the board has already been chosen. Flexographic, offset or lithographic, and digital printing each solve a different production problem. The right comparison is not “which method is best?” but “which method gives the required result on this surface, at this volume and with this artwork?”
Route Often considered when Confirm before approval
Flexographic printing The artwork is relatively direct and the order supports production setup. Number of colors, plate or setup charges, registration tolerance and kraft-versus-white appearance.
Offset or lithographic printing The brand needs detailed images, gradients or a more controlled coated print surface. How the printed sheet is laminated or mounted to the corrugated structure and how edges or folds affect the artwork.
Digital printing The project needs short runs, multiple versions or reduced setup for a launch or test. Substrate compatibility, color reference, variable artwork limits and whether the sample matches the bulk route.
State whether the outside print is CMYK, Pantone, a fixed number of spot colors or another agreed reference. Attach the final artwork to one identified dieline and define bleed, safe areas, crease direction, logo position and any no-print zones. A screen proof is useful for layout, but it is not automatically a physical color standard. If you want to compare construction and customization options for a specific pizza box, review the custom pizza packaging box product page and then ask every supplier to quote against the same selected options.

Part 4: Treat food contact as a finished-package system

“Food safe” is not a universal property that can be established by adding a food-grade ink checkbox. Suitability depends on the finished package, the food-facing surface, paper and board composition, coatings or treatments, inks, adhesives, intended food and time-temperature conditions, and the destination market. For an EU project, ask how the finished packaging is supported against the applicable food-contact framework and good manufacturing practice requirements. For a U.S. project involving paper or paperboard contacting aqueous or fatty foods, ask the supplier to identify the applicable basis for the intended use, including any limitations. These references guide the project; they do not replace a supplier's current documentation or a destination-specific review.
  • Identify the surface that touches the pizza and whether grease, moisture or steam is expected.
  • Ask which paper, liner, coating, ink and adhesive systems are used in the finished construction.
  • Confirm that the outside print does not create an unintended contact or transfer route when boxes are stacked.
  • Request current declarations, migration or suitability evidence where the market and intended use require it.
  • Record the intended food, temperature, contact time and delivery condition in the approval file.
Migration can involve direct contact, movement through the substrate, set-off between stacked printed surfaces or transfer through the gas phase. A supplier should therefore assess the complete construction and handling route rather than promise that one material is suitable for every pizza, oil level or market.

Part 5: Explain MOQ through setup, size and artwork splits

There is no universal MOQ for custom printed pizza boxes. A quoted minimum can be driven by board purchasing, machine setup, print plates, tooling, sheet yield, packing efficiency, production scheduling or the number of versions that must be kept separate. Ask each supplier to separate the commercial assumptions:
  • minimum quantity per size;
  • minimum quantity per artwork or flavor version;
  • whether quantities can be combined across compatible SKUs;
  • one-time plate, tooling, dieline or setup charges;
  • sample, proof and freight charges;
  • price breaks and the point at which the production route changes.
A buyer with several restaurant locations may reduce complexity by standardizing the board and closure while splitting artwork only where the brand requires it. Another buyer may need separate dimensions for different pizzas. The useful question is not “What is your MOQ?” in isolation; it is “What quantity, setup and SKU structure did you use to calculate this MOQ?”

Part 6: Turn the physical sample into an acceptance record

A digital proof cannot confirm every property of a pizza box. Approve a physical sample that follows the intended construction and, where practical, the intended print route. Keep the sample linked to the artwork version, dieline and quotation revision.
Sample check Record
Fit and closure Usable internal dimensions, lid alignment, corner clearance and closure force.
Board and conversion Liner appearance, flute direction, caliper reference, crease quality, glue and exposed edges.
Print Artwork version, logo position, color reference, registration, scuffing and print on folds.
Food-use simulation Condition after realistic hot, greasy or steamy use, with the test conditions written down.
Delivery handling Stacking, opening, carrying, movement and visible damage after the planned handling sequence.
Mark the sample as approved, approved with changes or rejected. Photograph any issue, note the responsible revision and do not let a later supplier sample silently replace the approved reference. A physical review improves purchasing control, but it is not a substitute for required laboratory, compliance or destination-market documentation.

Part 7: Freeze one RFQ before comparing suppliers

A comparable pizza-box quote starts with one frozen RFQ specification. Send the same information to every supplier and ask them to list deviations explicitly. If one supplier leaves a field blank, the quotation is not yet comparable.
RFQ field Required detail
Box and use Product type, usable inside dimensions, pizza height, clearance, hot/greasy/steamy condition and delivery route.
Board Outer liner, flute, inner liner, board grade or caliper, food-facing surface and any vent or liner requirement.
Print and artwork Print sides, CMYK/Pantone or other color basis, artwork file, dieline version, bleed and finishing.
Quantity Total units and exact quantity by size, artwork, location or SKU.
Sample route Blank structural sample, printed proof, production-route sample, test method, approval owner and revision control.
Compliance file Applicable food-contact documents, declarations or test evidence for the intended market and use.
Commercial output Unit price, setup and tooling, sample cost, packing quantity, lead-time assumptions, freight basis and quote validity.

Production handoff: send a specification, not a vague request

The handoff is ready when the supplier can answer the same frozen questions without inventing missing assumptions. Include the selected usable size, board direction, liner and flute, logo artwork, print route and color reference, food-contact condition, quantities by SKU, sample route, acceptance record and destination-market documentation needs. When quotations arrive, compare the assumptions line by line. A lower unit price may reflect a different liner, smaller clearance, fewer print colors, a different sample route or an MOQ that only works if several artworks are combined. Resolve those differences before comparing totals. That is the practical value of specification-first sourcing: the buyer can move from “I need logo pizza boxes” to a production-ready RFQ that multiple suppliers can price on materially comparable terms.
