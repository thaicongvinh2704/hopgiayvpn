Updated August 24, 2026: The United States does not have one simple federal rule that can be summarized as “all PFAS are banned from all food packaging.” The federal position is narrower: 35 Food Contact Notifications for PFAS-containing grease-proofers used on paper and paperboard are no longer effective, while multiple states separately prohibit intentionally added PFAS—or, in California, PFAS above a specified total-organic-fluorine threshold—in covered food packaging. If you searched for “PFAS food packaging ban news today,” the practical answer is therefore a jurisdiction-and-package test, not a yes-or-no headline. A paper box may satisfy a state PFAS restriction and still need a separate food-contact review. It may also be legally saleable but unable to support an unqualified “recyclable” or “PFAS-free” marketing claim. The decision rule: verify where the package will be sold, whether the finished article is within that jurisdiction’s definition of food packaging, what each component contains, and exactly which claim the evidence supports. Do not treat the appearance of brown kraft paper, a supplier’s generic “eco” statement, or one laboratory result as proof of all four questions.

Is there a nationwide ban on every PFAS in every food package? No single federal packaging rule has that scope. FDA’s packaging-specific action concerns the PFAS grease-proofing uses covered by 35 Food Contact Notifications. Do not use a federal headline as a substitute for a state and product-scope review.
What changed federally? The 35 relevant Food Contact Notifications became ineffective on January 6, 2025. FDA set June 30, 2025 as the compliance date for certain existing paper food-packaging stock. Confirm that no formerly authorized PFAS grease-proofer is used in the paper or paperboard construction.
Do state rules matter? Yes. State laws differ by material, direct-food-contact status, product category, definition of PFAS, threshold, certificate duty and effective date. Map every SKU to its destination states before approving material or artwork.
What is the most recent state change relevant to paper food packaging? Maine’s restriction for specified plant-fiber food-packaging categories took effect May 25, 2026, with defined scope and an exemption described by Maine DEP. Check whether the package, seller and food-use scenario fall within Maine’s rule rather than assuming every paper box is treated alike.
What about the August 12, 2026 PFAS deadline appearing in current news? That date relates to the European Union’s Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation, not a new U.S. nationwide food-packaging ban. Maintain separate U.S. and EU compliance specifications for products sold in both markets.
The key federal source is the FDA’s January 2025 determination. The agency states that the 35 notifications had authorized grease-proofing coatings for paper and paperboard and that the relevant uses were abandoned after manufacturers or suppliers ceased production, supply or use. The withdrawal of those authorizations is important, but it does not erase state-law differences or prove that every finished package contains zero detectable PFAS.

The federal action is narrower than a blanket PFAS ban

PFAS were used in some paper food-packaging applications because they could provide oil, grease and water resistance. FDA’s action addressed that food-contact use through the notification system. It did not create one universal definition, test threshold or sales prohibition covering every possible PFAS, every packaging material and every downstream claim. This distinction matters when a procurement team receives a statement such as “FDA-compliant PFAS-free paper.” Three questions are hidden inside that phrase:
  1. Authorization: Are all food-contact substances in the finished construction permitted for the intended conditions of use?
  2. PFAS status: Has PFAS been intentionally added to any component, and does the relevant jurisdiction also apply a concentration threshold?
  3. Claim support: Is the available evidence strong enough for the exact “free-of” wording printed on the box or used in sales materials?
FDA explains that the manufacturer of a food-contact substance is responsible for ensuring compliance with the specifications and limitations of applicable authorizations. Its guidance on the regulatory status of food-contact-material components directs reviewers to consider the substance identity, specifications and conditions-of-use limitations. A declaration about one barrier coating does not automatically clear the board, ink, adhesive, liner, window and every other food-contact component.

State rules decide whether a particular package can be sold

The safest way to read the U.S. state landscape is as a set of scope tests. The table below highlights representative rules that materially affect paper boxes and related fiber packaging; it is not a substitute for checking the current statute and agency guidance for every destination.

California Effective January 1, 2023 Plant-fiber-based food packaging is restricted when PFAS is intentionally added or when total organic fluorine is at or above 100 ppm, subject to the statutory definitions. A formulation declaration alone may not address the threshold. Align any test plan with California’s definition and the finished component being assessed.
New York Effective December 31, 2022 Food packaging intended for direct food contact and comprised mainly of paper, paperboard or other plant-derived material may not contain intentionally added PFAS. New York allows reliance on a supplier or manufacturer compliance certification. Retain the certification and identify the SKUs it covers.
Washington Phase 1: February 1, 2023; Phase 2: May 1, 2024 Specified plant-fiber categories include wraps, plates, food boats, pizza boxes, bags, sleeves, bowls, flat serviceware and open or closed containers. Manufacturers of restricted packaging must keep a certificate of compliance on file and provide it when requested.
Minnesota Effective January 1, 2024 The statute covers containers used to market, protect, handle, deliver, serve, contain or store food or beverages. Its definition includes unit, intermediate and shipping packages as well as assembled components such as coatings, closures, inks and labels. Do not assume a secondary or shipping package falls outside scope. Map every assembled part identified by the statute and verify that no PFAS was intentionally added.
Oregon Effective January 1, 2025 The restriction concerns foodware containers with intentionally added PFAS; it is not identical to a ban on every form of food packaging. Classify the article correctly before applying the Oregon rule to a carton, service container or secondary package.
Maine Effective May 25, 2026 Specified direct-contact, plant-fiber packaging used for short-term storage or freshly prepared food includes bakery boxes, pizza boxes, bags, sleeves, wraps, liners and several food-service containers. Document the packaging category, direct-contact use and applicability of any exemption instead of issuing a universal Maine statement for all boxes.
Official agencies illustrate why a single nationwide checklist is inadequate. New York DEC explains both its intentionally-added standard and its certification approach. The Washington Department of Ecology lists covered categories and certificate-retention requirements. Maine DEP defines the food-service categories covered from May 25, 2026. Other in-force state restrictions—including rules in Colorado, Connecticut, Hawaii, Maryland, Rhode Island and Vermont—also require destination-specific review.

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission’s Green Guides summary says an unqualified recyclable claim should be qualified when suitable recycling facilities are not available to at least 60% of consumers or communities where the product is sold. That marketing test is separate from whether the base sheet is technically made from recyclable fiber.

A PFAS-free barrier can still make a paper box harder to recycle

Replacing a fluorinated grease-proofing treatment is not the end of the design task. The substitute must still control oil or moisture under the intended food conditions, remain compatible with printing and converting, and support the desired end-of-life claim. This is the substitution trap: a coating can solve the PFAS question while adding a different challenge at the paper mill. A heavy film, difficult-to-separate layer, problematic adhesive or poorly repulpable treatment may reduce fiber recovery even when the coating contains no intentionally added PFAS. The American Forest & Paper Association’s recyclability guidance specifically asks designers and brands to evaluate the effect of non-fiber elements such as coatings and additives on paper-packaging recyclability. For coated corrugated products, AF&PA and the Fibre Box Association also maintain a voluntary protocol for evaluating repulpability and recyclability. Practical decision rule: first reject an unlawful or unsupported PFAS treatment; then compare the remaining barrier systems under the same food-use and converting conditions; finally evaluate the complete construction for the recycling claim you intend to make. “Fluorine-free,” “water-based” and “paper-based” are material descriptions, not automatic recycling outcomes.

PFAS status must be checked at component level

A finished food box is a system. Even when the paperboard is the largest component, the functional layer may be a coating, liner, lamination or converted surface treatment. The evidence request should follow the bill of materials rather than the visual appearance of the package.
Component PFAS question Performance question after substitution End-of-life question
Paper or paperboard Was any PFAS chemistry intentionally added during furnish preparation or surface treatment? Could recycled fiber introduce incidental background? Does the board retain stiffness, fold strength and printability for the actual box? Can the fiber be recovered in the intended recycling stream?
Grease or moisture barrier What chemistry creates the barrier, and is the supplier declaration tied to the exact grade? Does it resist the target food, temperature and contact time without cracking at scores? How does the coating affect repulping, screening and residue?
Ink, varnish or overprint coating Are fluorinated additives intentionally used in the specified ink or treatment? Does print adhesion remain acceptable on the new barrier surface? Does coverage or formulation change the finished-package assessment?
Adhesive Is the adhesive within the compliance declaration and the intended-contact construction? Does the seam hold after heat, cold, grease and humidity exposure? Does the adhesive disperse or separate acceptably in the relevant recycling process?
Window, liner or laminate Has the separate film or liner been included in the PFAS review? Is the barrier function coming from this layer rather than the paperboard? Can the component be separated, or is the combined structure accepted locally?
New York DEC makes a useful distinction: its law prohibits intentionally added PFAS, but incidental PFAS may still enter through processing aids or recycled content. That means “complies with New York’s intentionally-added restriction” and “contains no detectable PFAS” are not equivalent statements.

No single test result proves every PFAS claim

PFAS is a large class of substances, and analytical methods answer different questions. A targeted method measures the specific analytes included in that method. A total- or organic-fluorine approach can act as a broader indicator but may not identify which PFAS caused a result. A test performed on a base sheet also does not automatically describe the finished converted box. Testing should therefore follow the legal and commercial question:
  • For an intentionally-added prohibition: start with formulation and supply-chain declarations, because intent is a manufacturing fact. Use testing as part of a risk-based verification plan, not as a substitute for knowing the formulation.
  • For a concentration threshold: use a qualified laboratory, the required analyte or fluorine definition, suitable sample preparation and the reporting limit needed for that jurisdiction.
  • For a customer “PFAS-free” specification: define what “free” means, which components and lots are covered, the test method, analyte list, detection or reporting limits and treatment of incidental background.
  • For ongoing production: add supplier change control. A passing prototype is not evidence for future orders if the board, coating, ink or adhesive can change without notification.
A report should identify the sampled component or finished article, sample date, method, analytes or fluorine metric, reporting limits, results, laboratory and applicable specification. A certificate that says only “PFAS compliant” without a SKU, material grade, jurisdiction or date is too vague for a high-confidence approval.

The evidence pack that survives a buyer audit

For each food-packaging SKU, build one controlled file rather than collecting unrelated emails after a customer asks a question.
  1. Market and use statement: destination states, direct or indirect food contact, food type, temperature, contact duration and whether the article is primary, secondary or transport packaging.
  2. Finished construction: drawing or dieline plus a bill of materials listing board, coating, ink, varnish, adhesive, window, liner and insert.
  3. Supplier declarations: exact grade or product code, “no intentionally added PFAS” wording where appropriate, issue date, responsible signatory and the jurisdictions or customer specifications addressed.
  4. Food-contact documentation: compliance statements and conditions of use for relevant components. Do not replace this with a generic safety data sheet.
  5. Required certificates: state-specific certificates of compliance where applicable, linked to the products they cover and retained for the required period.
  6. Verification results: laboratory reports when required by law, customer specification or risk assessment, with a method capable of answering the stated question.
  7. Performance approval: barrier, fold, seam, print and pack trials under representative conditions.
  8. End-of-life support: evidence for repulpability, recyclability or compostability claims and the consumer qualification used in the target market.
  9. Change control: written notification requirements when a supplier changes a formulation, mill, grade or production route.

What to test after removing a PFAS grease barrier

A compliant substitute that fails in use can cause oil staining, loss of stiffness, opened glue seams, print damage or food leakage. The approval trial should reproduce the failure conditions of the actual package rather than relying only on a clean, room-temperature sample.
Use condition What to observe Why it affects the box decision
Hot, greasy food Oil strike-through, staining, odor transfer, panel softening and leakage over the expected contact period A barrier may work briefly but fail during delivery or holding time.
Score and fold lines Cracking or thinning of the barrier where the carton is creased and erected Flat-sheet data may not predict performance on a converted box.
Cold or humid storage Moisture uptake, board curl, delamination and loss of compression strength Humidity can change both barrier and structural performance.
Printed surface Ink adhesion, scuffing, drying and color appearance on the chosen coating A barrier substitution can change how the print system behaves.
Glue seam Bond formation and seam opening after representative exposure A surface that resists grease can also be difficult to bond unless the adhesive and application are compatible.
Recovery pathway Repulpability, rejects, non-fiber residue and acceptance in the target collection system Passing the food-use trial does not establish an unqualified recyclable claim.

Use environmental wording that matches the evidence

Proposed wording Main risk More defensible approach
“PFAS-free” Readers may understand an absolute absence claim across the whole package, while the evidence may cover only intentional addition, selected analytes or one component. Define the claim internally. Where accurate and supportable, “made without intentionally added PFAS” is more specific, but it still requires evidence for the relevant finished construction.
“FDA approved box” FDA regulates food-contact substances and their authorized conditions; this phrase can overstate what was reviewed. Identify the applicable food-contact compliance basis and conditions of use for the materials in the package.
“Recyclable paper box” Paper content alone may not establish practical recyclability of coatings, windows, liners, adhesives or food-contaminated packaging in the sales area. Assess the finished package and qualify the claim when collection or processing access is limited.
“Made with recycled paper” It can be mistaken for a recyclability claim and does not specify the verified recycled-content percentage. State the substantiated recycled-content percentage and whether it applies to a component or the whole package.
“Compostable” PFAS compliance and paper content do not prove compostability in home or industrial systems. Use claim-specific testing, certification and facility qualifications appropriate to the target market.
The same discipline should be applied to product pages, RFQs and artwork. A legal threshold, a food-contact authorization, a recycling test and a marketing claim each answer a different question. Combining them into one “eco-friendly” badge creates more risk than information.

A production-ready PFAS brief for custom paper boxes

When requesting custom printed paper boxes for food, send a brief that allows the converter to evaluate the complete construction:
  • destination country and every U.S. state where the packaged product will be sold;
  • direct, indirect or no food contact;
  • food type, grease level, serving temperature, storage condition and maximum contact time;
  • box structure, internal dimensions and whether the package is used for serving, short-term storage, retail display or transport;
  • paperboard grade, recycled-content requirement and every barrier, liner, window, ink, varnish and adhesive;
  • required PFAS wording: regulatory compliance, no intentionally added PFAS, a numerical threshold or a customer-specific restricted-substance list;
  • required certificate, laboratory method, analytes or fluorine metric, reporting limit and test frequency;
  • food-contact documentation and intended conditions of use;
  • recyclability or compostability claim, target collection system and required qualification;
  • sample tests and supplier change-control requirements.
