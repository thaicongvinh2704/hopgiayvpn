Not always. If “product weight” means net weight, packaging is not included. If it means gross weight or the actual weight of a packed shipment, the box, insert, wrapping and other packaging materials are included. Dimensional weight is different again: it is calculated from the outer size of the package rather than simply measured on a scale. This distinction matters because the same physical product can have several valid weight figures depending on whether you are preparing a product specification, designing packaging, creating a shipping label or estimating freight cost. A useful rule is: never use “product weight” as an isolated number in a packaging or shipping brief. State whether the number is net product weight, packed actual weight or shipping dimensional weight.

Net Weight, Gross Weight, Tare Weight and Dimensional Weight Compared

Weight term What it represents Does it include packaging? Typical use
Net weight The product or contents themselves No Product specifications, quantity declarations and product-level records
Tare weight The packaging or container without the product It is the packaging weight Separating packaging mass from product mass
Gross weight Net product plus the packaging included within the defined packed unit or shipment Yes Packed-product and logistics planning
Dimensional weight A calculated weight based on the outer volume of a parcel Not directly; the package dimensions determine it Parcel shipping rate calculations
Billable weight The weight figure a carrier uses to rate a shipment under its service rules Packaging can affect both actual and dimensional weight Freight and parcel charges
The basic relationship between net, tare and gross weight is: Gross Weight = Net Weight + Tare Weight Or, when the total packed weight is already known: Net Weight = Gross Weight − Tare Weight

So When Does Product Weight Include the Packaging?

The answer depends on what the person requesting the weight intends to do with it.

For a product specification: usually use net weight

If you are describing the product itself, the useful figure is normally the weight of the product without the shipping box or protective materials. For example, a device weighing 1.2 kg does not suddenly become a 1.7 kg product simply because it is packed inside a paper box, insert and corrugated mailer. The additional 0.5 kg belongs to the packaging system.

For a packed retail unit: include the packaging when the specification asks for packed weight

A buyer, warehouse or fulfillment team may need the weight of a complete sellable unit. In that case, the measurement should include the product and every component that remains with that unit, such as the retail carton, insert, manual or protective wrapping. The scope should still be stated clearly. “Packed unit weight: 1.38 kg” is more useful than simply writing “product weight: 1.38 kg.”

For a parcel shipment: weigh the complete packed parcel

When preparing a parcel for a carrier, the actual package weight is the weight of everything that will travel through the carrier network: the product, its retail packaging, protective insert or cushioning, outer shipping box and other packed components. This is one reason packaging engineers and sourcing teams should not work from product-only weight when designing ecommerce shipping packaging.

Packaging Can Change Shipping Weight in Two Different Ways

This is where the distinction becomes more important for ecommerce and export buyers. Packaging can affect shipping economics through two separate mechanisms:
  • Mass: adding paperboard, corrugated board, inserts and protective materials increases the actual weight placed on the scale.
  • Volume: increasing the outside length, width or height of the box can increase dimensional weight even when very little physical weight has been added.
The second effect is easy to overlook. A slightly heavier but tightly fitted structure may sometimes create a better shipping profile than an extremely light but oversized box.

How Dimensional Weight Is Different From Actual Weight

Dimensional weight, sometimes called DIM weight or volumetric weight, represents how much carrier space a parcel occupies relative to its physical weight. The general calculation is: Dimensional Weight = Length × Width × Height ÷ Carrier Divisor The dimensions are normally taken from the outside of the finished shipping package. The divisor and rounding rules depend on the carrier, country, service and rate agreement, so the applicable carrier rule should always be checked before final costing. For services that use a divisor of 5,000 cm³/kg, for example, a box measuring 40 × 30 × 20 cm would have a dimensional weight of: 40 × 30 × 20 ÷ 5,000 = 4.8 kg If the parcel physically weighs only 2.2 kg, the dimensional figure can still become the more important number for rating under a service that charges using the higher applicable weight. This means reducing packaging material alone does not necessarily optimize freight. Outer dimensions can matter as much as packaging mass.

A Packaging Example: Same Product, Different Shipping Weight

Consider an illustrative ecommerce packaging project. The figures below are an example for explaining the calculation, not a customer case.
Component Example weight
Product 1.20 kg
Retail paper box and insert 0.18 kg
Corrugated mailer, paper protection and shipping components 0.32 kg
Total actual packed weight 1.70 kg
The net product weight is still 1.20 kg. The additional 0.50 kg is packaging and therefore contributes to the packed gross or actual shipment weight. Now assume the first shipping-box design measures 38 × 28 × 18 cm. Using 5,000 cm³/kg as an illustrative divisor: 38 × 28 × 18 ÷ 5,000 = 3.83 kg dimensional weight The shipment weighs only 1.70 kg on the scale, yet its calculated dimensional weight is approximately 3.83 kg before any carrier-specific rounding. Suppose the packaging is redesigned around the real product and insert so the outside dimensions become 32 × 24 × 15 cm without reducing the required protection. The new calculation becomes: 32 × 24 × 15 ÷ 5,000 = 2.30 kg dimensional weight The net weight of the product has not changed. The physical product is still 1.20 kg. But the shipping profile has changed substantially because unnecessary package volume was removed. This is why a packaging quotation based only on product weight can miss an important part of the project.

Why a Lighter Box Is Not Automatically the Better Box

Reducing packaging mass can be useful, but “use the lightest packaging possible” is a poor standalone specification. A box still has to manage the product's weight, fragility, handling conditions, stacking, closure and shipping channel. Removing too much board or eliminating an insert may reduce tare weight while increasing movement or damage risk. The better target is the lowest practical packed weight and volume that still provides the required protection and presentation. For ecommerce orders, custom corrugated mailer boxes can be developed around the actual product dimensions, required protection and shipping format rather than forcing the product into an oversized stock carton. If the main question is whether the product should use a folding carton, rigid box or corrugated structure in the first place, the guide on choosing the right paper box structure for a product explains how product weight, fragility, inserts and sales channel affect that decision.

Which Weight Should You Give to Each Team?

A simple way to avoid specification errors is to send the weight that matches the task instead of one unlabeled “product weight” value.
Who needs the information? What to provide
Product or engineering team Net product weight plus dimensions
Packaging designer or manufacturer Net product weight, product dimensions, fragility and expected packed configuration
Warehouse or fulfillment team Complete packed-unit weight and external dimensions
Parcel carrier Actual packed parcel weight plus accurate outside length, width and height
Freight or export team Net and gross weight using the scope required by the shipment documentation
Regulated labeling or customs documentation The weight definition specifically required by the applicable market and document
For packaging development, providing both product-only and packed information is especially useful because the supplier needs to understand what the structure must carry before estimating the final box.

What to Send a Packaging Supplier Instead of Just “Product Weight”

A more production-ready packaging brief should contain:
  • Product dimensions: length × width × height.
  • Net product weight per unit.
  • Number of products inside each box.
  • Fragile areas or surfaces that require protection.
  • Required retail box or shipping-box format.
  • Insert, divider or cushioning requirements.
  • Target outer dimensions if fulfillment restrictions already exist.
  • Shipping method or sales channel.
  • Quantity, artwork status and destination market.
For a multi-unit pack, also specify whether the weight refers to one product, one finished retail set, one shipping carton or an entire palletized shipment. The word “gross” is only useful when everyone agrees on the boundary of the unit being measured.

Do Paper Boxes Add Enough Weight to Matter?

Sometimes the direct mass difference is small. Sometimes it is not. The result depends on box size, board construction, flute type, inserts, secondary packaging and the number of units being shipped. But focusing only on grams of paper can hide the larger issue. In parcel distribution, an oversized package can create a higher dimensional weight even when the additional paper itself is relatively light. For that reason, packaging optimization should normally examine these variables together:
  • Product protection.
  • Actual packed weight.
  • External box dimensions.
  • Empty space inside the package.
  • Insert efficiency.
  • Carton packing and units per master carton.
  • Applicable carrier or fulfillment rules.
A packaging structure that performs well is not simply the one with the lowest tare weight. It is the one that reaches an appropriate balance between protection, material use, outside dimensions and the distribution system it will enter.

The Practical Answer

Does the weight of a product include packaging? It depends on the weight definition being requested. Net weight excludes packaging. Gross or fully packed actual weight includes packaging. Dimensional weight is calculated from the outside dimensions of the package and can affect the shipping weight used for rating even when the physical package is relatively light. For packaging and logistics work, record at least two separate numbers whenever possible: product-only net weight and finished packed weight. For parcel shipping, record the finished external dimensions as well. If you are developing a new box, a custom packaging boxes manufacturer can work from the product dimensions, product weight, insert requirements and shipping method to define a structure that protects the product without treating packaging size and shipping efficiency as separate problems.
