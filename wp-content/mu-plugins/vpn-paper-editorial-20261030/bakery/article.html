Choose an SOS paper bag when a bakery order needs a bag that opens into a flat base for counter filling. Choose a long baguette paper bag when the main task is covering and carrying an elongated loaf. Then check the paper, grease resistance, ventilation and closure against the food and how long it will stay packed.

There is an important naming difference: SOS describes a construction, while baguette describes a product and bag format. They are not mutually exclusive technical categories. A supplier may offer a tall block-bottom bag for bread as well as a long flat or side-gusseted baguette sleeve. Ask for a drawing or an opened sample rather than ordering by name alone.

Look at the opened base before comparing the catalogue names

SOS is used for self-opening satchel, self-opening sack and self-opening square in different catalogues. The common practical feature is a folded base that opens into a flat footprint. The “square bottom” name does not mean that the footprint must be a geometric square. Here, SOS refers to the familiar open-top counter bag without handles.

A baguette bag is usually long and relatively narrow. It may be flat or have side gussets to accommodate the loaf. A flat bag has little expansion depth; gussets unfold to provide more room. Confirm whether the bottom is a flat seam or an opening block base, because that changes how the bag fills and stands. These distinctions follow the construction families shown by primary manufacturer TPBI in its SOS range and flat and satchel range.

Start with what happens at the bakery counter

The useful comparison is the packing task. A bag that displays well when empty may be awkward when staff have to load it quickly, fold the top and hand it to a customer.

Separate bag shape from grease and moisture performance

Brown kraft is a material description, not a guarantee that a bag resists oil, water or prolonged humidity. A plain bag that works for a dry roll may stain with a buttery pastry. A window that helps sell bread also introduces another material and a joining area that need to be specified.

For a greasy product, tell the supplier what touches the paper and how long it remains packed. Request a suitable grease-resistant paper or barrier construction and check the complete sample with the real product. For a window, identify the material and whether ventilation is required; “clear window” does not define its barrier or disposal properties.

Specify the window as a separate component: its material, position, dimensions, joining method and any required perforation. A paper-looking window and a clear polymer film are not interchangeable specifications. The combination of paper, window and adhesive also affects any environmental claim; assess the final construction and local collection requirements rather than printing a recycling message from appearance alone.

Warm bread adds another decision. Moisture released into a closed pack can change the eating quality and the appearance of the bag. An open sleeve, a ventilated window bag and a sealed barrier pack solve different problems. Run the trial at the bakery's real packing temperature and planned holding time; do not borrow a freshness claim from another bag.

That is evidence that the material system matters, not that all kraft bread bags have the same performance. Measure the bread and the packing action together

Use the largest normal product or combination you expect to pack, including any tray or inner wrap. Record width, thickness, length and filled weight. Agree the supplier's dimension convention in writing: the order of width, gusset and height varies between catalogues, and gusset dimensions can be interpreted differently.

For an SOS bag, check the opened internal footprint and usable height, including the space needed to fold the top. For a baguette bag, check the usable internal length, opening and expansion depth, then state how much of the loaf may remain exposed. The nominal height of a flat bag does not prove that the entire length remains usable around a rounded loaf and a closed bottom.

A useful fit trial has three steps: staff insert the real product without forcing it; they close or fold the top as planned; then they carry the filled pack in the intended way. Record where the paper catches, where the loaf presses against a seam and whether a window or printed panel ends up in the wrong place. Adjust the specification and repeat the trial before approving a large run.

Record the outcome at the time the customer will open it

Check immediately after packing and again at the intended end of the takeaway or display period. Record the product, its condition when packed, the closure and the elapsed time. Look for visible staining, a softened or damp area, separation around a window, a torn seam or damage to the food surface. These are practical observations to discuss with the supplier, not substitute laboratory results.

Do not use an immediate takeaway trial to approve several days of prepacked retail storage. That changes the packaging task and needs a product-specific shelf-life and material assessment. Likewise, a bag that works for a dry baguette does not automatically suit a warm filled sandwich or an oily pastry.

Avoid adding a universal allowance to every loaf. Required clearance changes with the bread shape, crust, filling method and closure. Our paper bag measuring guide provides the general measurement method; the filled trial settles the bakery-specific fit.

A bakery selling both products may need two specifications

Imagine a bakery that sells long baguettes alongside takeaway orders of several rolls. This is a planning example, not a customer case study. A large SOS bag might hold both, but it could leave a baguette poorly supported or create unnecessary empty volume. A narrow bread sleeve might suit the baguette but be inconvenient for several rolls.

Test one SOS specification for the multi-item order and one long bag for the baguette. Price them by quantity per size and design, not as a single combined production quantity. Ask staff to compare the two formats during a normal packing sequence, including where bundles are stored and how the bags are opened.

If you want to reduce the number of sizes, select the format that covers the most frequent real orders with acceptable fit. Keep a separate bag or box for the exceptions that fail the trial. Reducing SKUs is helpful only if the shared format still protects the product and works at the counter.

Ask for a bakery trial set before releasing the print order

Your request should name the bag construction and the food together. “Custom bread bag” is not enough to choose a base, paper or barrier. Send:

  • A photograph and dimensions of the bread or mixed order, including its filled weight.
  • The packing temperature and intended time before the customer opens the bag.
  • The preferred opening, base, window and top closure, with a reference if available.
  • Whether food touches the bag directly or uses an inner wrap.
  • Quantity per size and artwork, delivery country and required receiving date.

For direct food contact, ask for documents covering the actual paper, window, adhesive and other relevant components under the intended conditions. The FDA's food types and conditions of use distinguish, among other things, fatty bakery products and different temperature/storage conditions. A successful fit or grease trial does not establish regulatory compliance.
