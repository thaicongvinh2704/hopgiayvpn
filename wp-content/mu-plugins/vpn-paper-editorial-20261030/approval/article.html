“Approved, but please move the logo and use stronger handles” is not a complete production instruction. It approves a sample while changing two of its features. Before a large paper bag order proceeds, the buyer and supplier need to know which version is approved and what still needs to be proved.

Paper bag sample approval should record the accepted specification, the evidence reviewed, any permitted differences and who authorises production. Check the complete filled bag as well as the artwork. A screen proof can confirm layout and text; it cannot establish the finished bag's carrying performance or physical colour.

First identify what the sample can actually prove

Ask how the sample was made and list differences from the proposed bulk process. It may use substitute paper, hand assembly, a different printing method or an existing handle component. Such a sample can still answer a useful question, but only if its limits are understood.

Review three kinds of evidence separately:

  • Artwork evidence: the correct file revision, spelling, panel allocation, logo position and finish areas.
  • Physical evidence: the bag's size, materials, opening, folds, handles, base and fit with the intended contents.
  • Production evidence: which approved materials and processes will be used, how the order will be inspected and which reference inspectors will follow.

A plain structural sample cannot settle printed colour. A printed sheet cannot settle a handle attachment. A finished sample made by a different process may not settle the appearance of the production run. Request the missing evidence before approving a feature that the current sample cannot demonstrate.

For example, a buyer might accept the size of sample S02 but hold print release because artwork R04 has not been checked. Record those as two separate decisions. “Dimensions accepted; printing not released pending R04 approval” gives production a clearer boundary than “approved with changes.” The identifiers here are illustrative.

Inspect the bag as it will be filled and carried

Opening, gusset and usable space

Pack the actual item, including its retail box, tissue, insert or wrap. Open the gussets fully and check the bottom. Look for forced corners, a tight mouth, a tilted base or contents that push into a fold. For an open-top bakery bag, include the planned top fold or closure in the fit check.

Record the measured result, the reference points and the agreed tolerance. State whether dimensions are internal or external and whether height excludes the handles. A photograph of the ruler position can remove ambiguity. Use the paper bag size guide for measurement basics, then put the agreed method into this order's specification.

Handles, attachments and base

For a carrier, inspect both handles and their attachments, including patches or reinforcement where specified. Look for lifting edges, tears, loose knots where relevant or uneven positioning. Check the base folds and seams for gaps, paper damage and visible separation.

Agree a functional trial based on the actual contents and handling route. Define the load, how it is distributed, the lifting or carrying sequence, environmental conditions and what counts as failure. A quick lift in the office is useful feedback, but it is not a certified load rating. The industry initiative The Paper Bag identifies EN 13590 as a carrier-bag performance reference; use a suitable agreed test when a formal rating is required. This article does not claim that VPN's bags hold that certification.

Handle-free bags need their own assessment of the base, side seams and intended handover method. Applying a carrier-handle test to a baguette sleeve would not answer the same question. If a bag fails, document where it failed rather than increasing paper weight without investigating the construction.

Paper and surface

Match the sample to the specified paper grade, colour, grammage, coating and reinforcement where applicable. A similar hand-feel does not establish that two papers are equivalent. Ask for material identification and note any substitution used in the sample. Review fold cracking, rubbing and visible marks under agreed viewing conditions.

Approve print on the assembled bag

Lay the artwork proof beside the physical bag. Check the front, back, gussets and base where they are printed. A logo can be centred on a flat panel yet appear off-centre after the side seam and folds are formed. Important text should remain readable on the filled bag, including near the top fold and handle attachment.

For colour-sensitive work, name the physical target and viewing conditions. Brown or coloured paper can change the appearance of an ink, and a screen image is not a physical colour standard. If you agree a measured colour tolerance, both parties need access to the agreed method. Do not add a numerical limit simply because it looks precise.

Inspect foil, embossing, coatings or lamination only where they belong in the specification. Check their position and behaviour at folds. Our paper bag printing guide covers process and artwork choices; this review is about accepting the result that production must reproduce.

For bags in direct contact with food, keep the food-contact documentation review separate from visual approval. Request documents relevant to the final materials, components and intended use. The FDA's component-status guidance explains why the finished article's composition matters in the United States. A photograph, factory tour or clean-looking sample cannot establish that status.

Convert comments into a release record

Give each open issue an outcome: approved as shown, approved only for a named feature, or held pending new evidence. If a required feature is still open, do not label the whole order “approved” unless the written release clearly limits what may proceed. Commercial consequences and authorisation should be agreed by the buyer and supplier.

This example shows why one “yes” cannot cover every feature. A buyer can accept the size while print remains on hold. Keep the approved items linked to their sample and drawing, and identify which later revision supersedes an earlier one.

Describe a defect with a location and observation: “left handle patch lifts at its lower edge during the agreed trial” is actionable; “handle quality poor” is not. The proposed correction might affect the patch, adhesive, paper or application method. Agree which correction will be made and how the revised attachment will be checked instead of accepting a promise to make it stronger.

The release document should contain the purchase order, SKU, sample ID, dated specification and artwork revisions, material references, approved inspection requirements, open deviations, responsible approver and release date. If approval is limited to a component or process stage, state that explicitly.

Decide which changes require another check

The question is not whether every change needs an entirely new sample. It is whether the evidence you already approved still represents the changed feature and the features it affects.

  • Artwork position only: a revised proof may settle placement, provided it uses the approved structure and does not introduce another print or finish issue.
  • Different paper, coating or reinforcement: revisit appearance, folds, bonding and functional requirements affected by that material.
  • Changed gusset, base or overall size: repeat the relevant fit and assembly checks; the earlier filled sample may no longer represent the bag.
  • Changed handle or attachment: review the assembled connection and repeat the agreed handling assessment.
  • Different food-contact component: review relevant supporting documentation and performance requirements again.

For an urgent correction, write down exactly what can proceed and what must wait. A verbal instruction such as “make the same bag, just stronger” leaves paper, reinforcement and construction open to interpretation. Confirm the proposed correction, the evidence needed to accept it and any change to price or schedule.

Keep sampling, bulk inspection and receiving linked

Retain a dated physical reference with the buyer and supplier where practical, together with the final files and approval record. Confirm whether the reference is the actual approved sample or an agreed duplicate. Label superseded versions so they cannot be mistaken for the production standard.

Sample approval does not show that every unit in a large order will match. Agree the production inspection plan separately: checks, stage, sample selection, acceptance criteria, reporting and who can release the shipment. Use a contractual sampling method where required rather than assuming a single sample represents the entire lot.

At receiving, compare the shipment's SKU and carton labels with the approved order, then record any mismatch using identifiable cartons, photographs and measured observations. These records let the supplier investigate a specific issue instead of debating what “different from the sample” means.
