If you buy single-serve bags for a bakery counter or café chain, use this guide to decide whether the customer needs to see the cookie through the bag and how the bag must look at the end of service. Trial glassine first for a cookie the customer should be able to recognize. Compare a specified greaseproof grade when visible oil marks or transfer are a purchasing concern. Test both finished bags with the same recipe, packing temperature and sale window. Thomas & Green’s paper guide describes the appearance and uses of glassine.

Match the bag to the cookie and selling window

Cooled cookie sold by the piece
Sample flat glassine. Check usable opening width with the widest baked cookie and the planned fold.
Thicker item or multiple cookies
Compare a gusseted bag. Check insertion, opened depth and seam behavior on a filled bag.
Visible oil or warm service
Compare specified paper grades on the same recipe and hold time. Request food-contact evidence for the intended use and market.

Start with the food and the selling window

Use a cooled biscuit, a buttery cookie and a filled pastry as separate tests if the same bag may serve all three. Surface fat, crumbs and moisture affect appearance differently. Record how hot or cool each item is at packing, how long it stays in the bag and whether it travels on a tray, lies flat or sits upright in delivery. Testing a plain biscuit alone leaves the richer recipes unrepresented.

Choose a flat bag, gusseted bag or bakery carton

Flat glassine bag: start here for a single thin cookie sold at the counter. Check the mouth opening and the fold allowance after loading. Gusseted bag: trial the extra room when the pack contains several cookies or a thicker item. Confirm the expanded depth and whether the seams stay flat. Carton with a liner: sample this when a soft topping needs clearance or the pastry needs more support in transit.

Flat and gusseted glassine bags can use different size conventions. The Castaway foodservice catalogue lists both formats. Ask the supplier to mark usable width, opened gusset and cut length separately on the drawing.

Compare glassine and greaseproof paper by grade

Put visual clarity and grease performance on separate lines in the specification. A translucent finish helps for display, while an oil-prone recipe may call for a grade sold for a particular level of grease resistance. Ask for the exact paper specification and the test method behind any resistance claim. Compare both papers after converting, because folds and seam construction can change what the bakery sees. For grade selection on higher-oil foods, see the wholesale greaseproof bakery bag guide.

When the bag touches food directly, tell the supplier the food type, fat exposure, packing and storage temperatures, contact time, destination and whether a separate liner is used. Ask which supporting documents cover the finished construction, including the paper, inks and adhesive where relevant. The FDA’s reference tables for U.S. food-contact reviews distinguish food types and conditions of use.

Measure the bag around the way you pack

Measure the widest item and the thickness of the full pack-out. Record whether staff slide one cookie in flat or stack several together. Ask for usable width and depth after seams and folds, with extra length for the planned fold, label or closure. Trial a gusset if the bag needs to open for a stack. Staff should be able to load a full portion quickly with room to fold or close it.

Review a printed proof with a real cookie inside. Translucency changes the visible contrast behind a logo. Read the artwork over the lightest and darkest products and put required copy on an area with consistent contrast. After a handling trial, check rub, creasing and whether the label stays down.

Example: size a single-cookie bag for counter service

For a cookie measuring up to 85 mm across and 16 mm thick, trial a flat bag with 100 mm of usable width as a starting sample. Check the widest and thickest cookies through the actual opening: filling a flat bag changes the usable room at the mouth. If the paper catches the topping or split crumbs lodge in the fold, sample a wider opening or a gusset. Then verify that the closed bag fits the shelf label and service tray.

Run a finished bag test

  1. Use the highest-oil recipe in the range and the actual service temperature. Pack it as staff would during a shift.
  2. Inspect the front, back, folds and seams after the intended selling or delivery window. Check for transfer to the bag underneath.
  3. Repeat with the palest and darkest products to compare label legibility and display visibility.
  4. Record the paper grade, print version, dimensions, closure and acceptance criteria on the sample you retain.

Use the same hold time for both bag samples

If a cookie normally sits in the display for two hours, compare both bags after two hours under the same lighting and stacking conditions. Grade oil transfer onto an adjacent pack separately from a visible mark on the bag itself. Agree with the buyer which of those marks is acceptable before comparing grades on price.

Score the samples against the same conditions. Record recipe, packing temperature, elapsed time, display position, face marks, seam marks, transfer to an adjacent pack, and readability. Use observable criteria agreed with the bakery, such as whether oil transfers to the pack underneath at the end of the planned sale window. A test in the bakery screens appearance and usability. Confirm required material documents and any applicable laboratory testing separately.

Common buyer questions about glassine

Will the customer see the cookie clearly?

Glassine generally softens the view. Paper grade, print coverage and the cookie color all affect visibility. Compare a bare proof and a filled bag under the lighting at the counter.

Can one bag work across several recipes?

Start with the warmest and oiliest recipe, then check the widest product. Share both pack-outs with the supplier if they represent different conditions.

For a printed bag, write down which visible marks the bakery accepts at the end of the display period. If the cookie is clearly visible but oil transfers to the pack below, compare another grade using the same fold, dimensions and service conditions. Repeat on the finished printed bag before release.
