How many paper bags fit in a container? Calculate from the number of bags in each packed carton, the carton’s outside dimensions and gross weight, and an agreed loading arrangement. The container’s advertised cubic capacity alone cannot tell you the answer. Ribbon handles, base cards, bag size, packing protection and pallets can change the result even when the purchase order contains the same number of bags.

For an importer or distributor, the useful answer is a loading estimate tied to a specific packing revision. That estimate should tell you how many saleable bags arrive, how they are identified, and whether your warehouse can receive them. This guide shows how to build it without treating a theoretical volume calculation as a confirmed shipment.

Ask for the packed specification before a container quantity

An opened shopping bag is a poor starting point for freight calculations. Bags travel folded, nested or bundled. A deep gusset may fold flat, while a ribbon knot, rigid base insert or delicate surface can create local thickness that prevents a bundle from compressing evenly. Changing the protective packing can therefore change freight volume without changing the bag’s finished dimensions.

Request a packing record for each size and artwork. If production has not started, label the figures as an estimate and ask which will be checked on a completed trial carton. Keep both the original estimate and the later confirmed record so a change in shipment volume is visible.

Calculate carton CBM and the volume of the order

CBM means cubic metres. With carton dimensions in metres, multiply length by width by height. With measurements in centimetres, divide the product by 1,000,000. Measure the sealed outside carton; the internal dimensions of the box omit board thickness and any outward deformation.

Illustrative example: a sealed carton measures 50 × 40 × 30 cm and contains 500 bags. Its volume is 0.50 × 0.40 × 0.30 = 0.060 m³. An order of 100,000 bags requires 200 such cartons and occupies 12 m³ before pallet materials or other loading allowances. These are invented planning inputs, not a VPN packing specification or a container-capacity promise.

If a second specification holds the same 500 bags in a 50 × 40 × 36 cm carton, each carton occupies 0.072 m³. The same bag quantity then needs 14.4 m³, an additional 2.4 m³. That difference is a reason to review packing, not permission to squeeze the bags into a smaller carton and damage the handles or finish.

Ask what created the increase: a thicker paper, a changed handle, more space around a foil surface, or simply a different bundle arrangement. Compare complete packing specifications rather than asking a supplier to match another company’s carton count.

Use volume division as a first check, then test the geometry

Dividing available cargo volume by carton CBM gives a screening estimate. It does not account for the rectangular shape of the cartons, spaces between them, the container door, floor condition, blocking or the loading sequence. An additional centimetre on the carton can remove an entire row from a particular arrangement.

For a simple, single-orientation layout, count cartons along the usable length, across the usable width and through the permitted stack height. Round each count down before multiplying. The usable dimensions must come from the proposed equipment and loading plan, not a generic capacity table.

For example, suppose a hypothetical clear loading space is 5.8 × 2.2 × 2.2 m. Using the 0.50 × 0.40 × 0.30 m cartons in that orientation gives 11 × 5 × 7 = 385 cartons, or 192,500 bags. Pure volume division gives approximately 467 cartons. The gap demonstrates unused space; neither figure is a confirmed loading quantity. Mixed orientations might improve the arrangement, but stacking strength, permitted orientation and safe unloading still require review.

Maersk lists distinct dry and high-cube container types. Ask the forwarder to confirm the actual equipment dimensions, door opening and applicable limits. Do not use “40-foot” as a complete loading specification.

Check weight separately, even for bulky paper bags

Multiply the measured carton gross weight by the proposed carton count, then add pallets and other packing materials as applicable. Check this against the allowable cargo weight for the actual movement. Equipment limits, route restrictions and the distribution of the load must be reviewed by the responsible logistics team.

Some bag orders run out of space first; a dense paper specification or mixed cargo can behave differently. There is no useful rule that paper bags are always volume-limited. A lighter carton also does not automatically permit a higher stack: crushing can be governed by carton construction, storage conditions and the way the load is supported.

Choose pallets or floor loading around the receiving operation

Pallets can simplify handling, but their footprint and height consume space. Floor loading can use a different arrangement, while requiring more manual carton handling at receipt. The appropriate choice depends on your receiving equipment, labour, protection requirements and delivery rules. There is no fixed percentage of extra bags that floor loading always provides.

The IMO/ILO/UNECE CTU Code provides guidance on packing and securing cargo transport units. A packing calculation supports that work; it does not replace a safe stowage and securing plan.

Build a mixed-SKU order from carton lines

A container total can hide the wrong stock mix. Separate each bag size, construction and artwork on the packing list. Multiply each line’s carton count by its own CBM and gross weight, then add the lines. Keep partial cartons visible instead of quietly rounding them into full packs.

A buyer might need 60,000 small bags and 40,000 large bags. Using the small bag’s pack count for the whole order would overstate the quantity if the large format packs fewer units per carton. The same problem appears when a seasonal artwork needs separate protective packing or when branches require pre-sorted assortments.

For bakery programmes, identify the bread and bakery bag formats separately. A long baguette sleeve and a square-bottom carrier may share kraft paper but have different bundle shapes. Ask for the carton schedule before combining them into one freight estimate.

Protect the approved packing revision

Once a loading estimate has been accepted, record the carton dimensions, unit count, packing materials and orientation that produced it. If the specification changes, ask for an updated estimate. A revised ribbon attachment, added base card or thicker bundle separator can invalidate the earlier result.

Before dispatch, compare the final packing list with the loading schedule. Record carton quantities by SKU and photograph the agreed identification and packing arrangement. The aim is to connect what was manufactured with what the warehouse expects to receive, rather than relying on an attractive photograph of a full container.

Send a brief that can produce a useful loading estimate

For a custom order, send the bag dimensions, construction, artwork split, quantities by SKU, delivery destination and any warehouse pallet rules. Include whether carton assortments must match individual branches. Ask for bags per carton, outside carton dimensions and carton gross weight as separate quotation inputs.

Container-planning questions

Can I use a supplier’s previous bags-per-container figure?

Use it only as a reference until bag construction, carton count, outside dimensions, weight and loading method are confirmed to match. A previous shipment of a different size is not a capacity guarantee for the next order.

Should the cheapest unit price decide which quotation wins?

Compare the agreed bag specification, order split and packing first. Then compare product and logistics charges on the same scope. A lower bag price can come with a larger packing volume or a stock mix your warehouse cannot use efficiently.

Does a larger container always lower the cost per bag?

No. Ask for current quotations for the actual route and quantity. Unused capacity, receiving restrictions and the timing of replenishment can change the decision. This guide does not provide freight rates or a universal FCL/LCL break-even point.

What should be confirmed after a packing trial?

Confirm the count and outside dimensions of a sealed carton, gross weight, protection, carton identification and the permitted handling arrangement. Update the loading calculation whenever one of those inputs changes.
